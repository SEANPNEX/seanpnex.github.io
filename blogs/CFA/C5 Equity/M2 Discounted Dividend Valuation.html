Discounted Dividend Valuation ​

Choosing which cash flow to discount (dividends, free cash flow or residual income); the Gordon growth model, PVGO and justified P/E; multistage dividend discount models (two-stage, H-model, three-stage, spreadsheet); implied growth and implied required return; and sustainable growth.

Choosing the Cash Flow ​

Cash flowMost suitable whenProblems
DividendsThe company pays dividends; the board's policy has a clear, consistent link to profitability; a non-control (minority) perspectiveNon-payers; dividends unrelated to earnings
FCFE / FCFFNo dividends, or dividends far from FCFE; FCF tracks profitability within a comfortable forecast horizon; a control perspective (also when a takeover is possible)Heavy capex → FCF negative for many years
Residual incomeNo dividends, and FCF negative within the comfortable horizonNeeds detailed accrual accounting; distorted by poor accounting quality
  • FCFF = CFO − capex (including working capital): the cash flow before debt. Equity = PV(FCFF) − market value of debt. FCFF is easier when leverage is expected to change a lot.
  • FCFE = FCFF − net payments to debtholders (interest and principal, net of new debt): the cash flow after debt.
  • Residual income = earnings − required return × beginning book value; value = book value + PV(future RI). RI usually recognizes value earlier than FCF models. It relies on clean surplus: Bt=Bt−1+Et−Dt.

Example (AB InBev vs Diageo). AB InBev's payout ranged from 18% to 542% (acquisitions, a 2018 dividend cut) → a DDM is not appropriate; use a company-level cash flow. Diageo's EPS and DPS grew at similar rates (6.8% vs 6.3%), with a payout mostly 50–60% → a DDM is appropriate.

Dividend Discount Model ​

For one period, n periods and forever:

V0=D1+P11+rV0=∑t=1nDt(1+r)t+Pn(1+r)nV0=∑t=1∞Dt(1+r)t

Dividends are assumed to be paid once, at the end of each period. Even an investor with a finite horizon depends on all future dividends, through the sale price Pn.

Examples.

  • Carrefour (one period): D1 = €0.46, P1 = €23.00, r = 8% → V0 = 23.46/1.08 = €21.72.
  • Five-year horizon: dividends $2.00, 2.10, 2.20, 3.50, 3.75; P5 = $40; r = 10% → PV of dividends 9.926 + PV of price 24.837 = $34.76.

There are two ways to make the infinite sum workable: (1) assume a stylized growth pattern (constant, two-stage, H-model, three-stage); or (2) forecast individual dividends for 3–10 years (depending on how visible earnings are), then add a terminal value from a growth model or a multiple.

Gordon Growth Model ​

V0=D0(1+g)r−g=D1r−g(r>g)

It assumes dividends grow at a constant rate g forever, with long-term r and g, and r>g. Value, dividends and (with a constant payout) earnings all grow at g, so g is also the capital gains yield, and the dividend yield stays constant.

IMPORTANT

Use D1, not D0, in the numerator. Use the GGM only when long-run g is at most nominal GDP growth (real growth + expected inflation); if expected growth is well above that, use a multistage model.

Example (Sonoco). D0 = $1.64 (4 × $0.41), g = 4.5%, r = 3% + 0.95(4.5%) = 7.3% → V0 = 1.64(1.045)/0.028 = $61.21. The price is $59.55 → slightly undervalued.

Example (Middlesex Water). D0 = $0.911, g = 4.5%, price $43.20.

Estimate of rV0Verdict
Analyst: 6.8%$41.39Within 5% → fairly valued
CAPM, adjusted β 0.80: 3% + 0.8(4.5%) = 6.6%$45.33Fairly valued (about 5% above)

The GGM fits Middlesex: a stable ROE (a regulated water utility), a consistent 60–70% payout, growth of 4.5% close to US nominal GDP growth (about 4.3%), and no period of abnormal growth. Its beta has an R2 below 20%, so the CAPM r is uncertain.

Sensitivity. Moving r and g by ±25 bp gives values from $33.92 to $53.02; value explodes as r−g shrinks.

Other uses of the formula.

  • Perpetual preferred stock (non-callable, fixed rate): V0=D/r. E.g., a 4% preferred with $25 par pays $1.00; at r = 5.5%, V0 = $18.18.
  • Negative growth works too. E.g., Afton Mines: D1 = $4.25, g = −4%, r = 9% → 4.25/0.13 = $32.69.
StrengthsLimitations
Simple, easy to communicateVery sensitive to r and g
Fits mature, stable dividend payers and broad developed-market indexesUnusable for non-payers or unpredictable dividends
Gives the terminal value in multistage modelsInvalid if g≥r; unrealistic for high-growth companies
Can back out implied g or r

Implied Growth and Share Repurchases ​

  • Buybacks reduce the share count, but companies don't commit to buyback levels and time them opportunistically, so they're harder to forecast than dividends. At market prices, buybacks are neutral for remaining shareholders. A DDM still works if per-share dividend growth reflects the buybacks.
  • The implied growth rate solves P0=D0(1+g)/(r−g) for g:
g=rP0−D0P0+D0

Example. Beta 1.1, rf 5.6%, ERP 6% → r = 12.2%. D0 = $2.00, g = 5% → V0 = 2.10/0.072 = $29.17, below the $40 price. Implied growth: 40=2(1+g)/(0.122−g) → g = 6.86%: the market prices in more growth than 5%.

PVGO and Justified P/E ​

A share's value is the value of its assets in place, plus the present value of its growth opportunities:

V0=E1r+PVGOP0E1=1r+PVGOE1
  • E1/r is the no-growth value. A no-growth company has no positive-NPV projects, should pay out all its earnings, and its earnings stay flat.
  • Growth adds value only if new investment earns more than r (ROE > r). Reinvesting below r raises EPS but destroys value.
  • PVGO comes from investment opportunities and real options (timing, scale, abandonment).
  • A negative PVGO (e.g., Macy's, −$36.69 in early 2019) means the market expects value-destroying reinvestment, or that E1 is too high or r too low. By contrast, Alphabet's PVGO was 53% of its price and McDonald's 20%.

Example (Middlesex Water). E1 = $1.52, r = 6.8%, price $43.20 → E1/r = 22.42, so PVGO = $20.78 (48% of the price). P/E 28.4 = no-growth P/E (1/0.068 = 14.7) + growth component (20.78/1.52 = 13.67).

Justified P/E from the GGM, where b is the retention ratio and 1−b the payout:

Leading: P0E1=1−br−gTrailing: P0E0=(1−b)(1+g)r−g

For multistage models, the justified leading P/E is the model value ÷ E1.

Example (L'Oréal). Price €242.70, E0 €7.08, D0 €3.85, g 4.25%, r = 2% + 0.72(5%) = 5.6%, payout 0.5438.

  • Leading P/E = 0.5438/(0.056 − 0.0425) = 40.28; trailing = 0.5438(1.0425)/0.0135 = 42.00.
  • The actual trailing P/E is 242.70/7.08 = 34.3, below 42.0 → undervalued; the GGM value of €297.31 is above €242.70.

Two-Stage Model ​

V0=∑t=1nD0(1+gS)t(1+r)t+Vn(1+r)nVn=Dn+1r−gL=D0(1+gS)n(1+gL)r−gL

Steps: (1) list D1,…,Dn; (2) Dn+1=Dn(1+gL); (3) Vn=Dn+1/(r−gL); (4) discount everything at r. It fits a temporary advantage (a patent, first-mover status); its weakness is the abrupt drop in growth.

Example (Carl Zeiss Meditec). D0 €0.55; growth 9% for 10 years, then 5%; r = 1.2% + 0.9(5.2%) = 5.88%; price €80.55.

  • D10 = €1.302 and D11 = €1.3671, so V10 = 1.3671/0.0088 = €155.36, with a PV of 155.36/1.0588¹⁰ = €87.74.
  • PV of the 10 dividends €6.47 → V0 = €94.21 (about 93% from the terminal value).

Terminal value can come from (1) the GGM on Dn+1, or (2) a multiple × a forecast fundamental (P/E × EPS, P/B × BVPS).

Example (P&G, DDM with a P/E terminal value). D0 $2.79, growth 4% for 4 years, r 6.5%; at year 4, payout 60% and trailing P/E 22.

  • D4 = 2.79(1.04)⁴ = $3.2639; EPS₄ = 3.2639/0.60 = $5.4398; V4 = 22 × 5.4398 = $119.68, with a PV of $93.03.
  • PV of dividends $10.52 → V0 = $103.55 (price $96.47).

Example (a company that doesn't pay yet). First dividend $1.00 at t = 5, then 5% growth; r = 11%. V4=D5/(r−g) = 1.00/0.06 = $16.67 → V0 = 16.67/1.11⁴ = $10.98.

IMPORTANT

Vn=Dn+1/(r−g). If the first dividend is at t = 5, the GGM gives the value at t = 4, so discount 4 years, not 5.

H-Model ​

Growth starts at gS and declines linearly to gL over 2H years:

V0=D0(1+gL)r−gL+D0H(gS−gL)r−gL
  • The first term is the value if growth were gL from now; the second is the extra value from above-normal growth. It is an approximation, less accurate for a large H or a large gS−gL.
  • H is half the length of the high-growth period (10 years → H = 5).

Examples.

  • Meditec as an H-model (9% declining to 5% over 10 years): V0 = [0.55(1.05) + 0.55(5)(0.04)]/0.0088 = €78.13, against €94.21 from the two-stage model.
  • Price €41.70, D0 €1.77, 7% declining to 4% over 10 years, r 8%: V0 = [1.77(1.04) + 1.77(5)(0.03)]/0.04 = (1.84 + 0.27)/0.04 = €52.75. Normal growth alone gives 1.84/0.04 = €46, and the above-normal growth adds €6.75. The value is 26% above the price → undervalued.

  • V0 = $222.82, more than 14% above the price; the terminal value is about 88% of the value.

Version 2: a constant first stage, then an H-model for stages 2 and 3. Steps: (1) PV of the stage-1 dividends; (2) the H-model value at the start of stage 2, using Dn; (3) discount that to t = 0; (4) add.

Example (Rhinestone Energy). Price $56.18, D0 $0.56; growth 11% for 5 years, then declining linearly to 6.5% over 10 years (H = 5); r = 3% + 1.2(4.2%) = 8%; fair-value band ±20%.

  • D5 = 0.56(1.11)⁵ = $0.9436.
  • V5 = 0.9436(1.065)/0.015 + 0.9436(5)(0.045)/0.015 = 66.9979 + 14.1545 = $81.1524, with a PV of $55.2310.
  • PV of the stage-1 dividends $3.0422 → V0 = $58.27 (stage 1 is 5.2% of the value; stages 2–3 are 94.8%).
  • The band $46.62–$69.92 contains $56.18 → fairly valued.
  • With a flat dividend in year 2: D5 = $0.8501, V5 = $73.1103 → V0 = $52.56.

Bloomberg uses version 2 with growth plus transition fixed at 17 years: explosive 3/14, high 5/12, average 7/10, slow/mature 9/8.

Choosing a Model ​

ModelUse when
GGMMature; growth at or below nominal GDP; stable payout
Two-stageHigh growth for a set period, then an abrupt fall (e.g., patent expiry)
H-modelHigh growth declining gradually to maturity
Three-stageDistinct growth, transition and mature phases (younger companies)
SpreadsheetAny irregular pattern (asset sales, special dividends, detailed forecasts)

Example (Yang Co., irregular dividends). D1 $21; then −10% a year for 3 years (18.90, 17.01, 15.31); $60 in year 5 (an asset sale); $40 in years 6–7; then +5%; r 12%.

  • V7 = 40(1.05)/0.07 = $600, with a PV of $271.41; the PV of the dividends is about $128.06.
  • V0 = $399.48.

Limitations of multistage models: the terminal value is often over 75% of the value and very sensitive to r and g, and the life-cycle picture is crude when technology changes fast.

Spreadsheet Modeling ​

Forecast sales, margins, interest, tax, payout and the balance sheet → dividends → discount them, with a terminal value from a multiple.

Example (Hoshino Distributors). Sales $100m growing 20%, 15%, 10%, 10%; EBIT margin 20/20/18/16/16%; interest 10% of debt; tax 40%; payout 20/20/30/40/50%; assets 80% of sales; 4m shares; r 15%; terminal P/E 10.

Required Return from a DDM ​

Given the price, the required return is the IRR the model implies. For the GGM and the H-model:

r=D1P0+gr=D0P0[(1+gL)+H(gS−gL)]+gL

Examples.

  • NextEra: D1 = $5.00(1.055) = $5.275, P0 $169.83, g 5.5% → r = 3.11% + 5.50% = 8.60%.
  • GGM: price $10, D1 $0.50, g 8% → 13%.
  • H-model: D0 $1, price $20, 10% declining to 6% over 10 years (H = 5): r = (1/20)[1.06 + 5(0.04)] + 0.06 = 12.3%.

For other multistage models, find r by trial and error (or Goal Seek) so that the PV equals the price.

Example (J&J). D0 $3.60, growth 7% for 6 years then 5%, price $136.61. The GGM first guess is 3.60(1.07)/136.61 + 0.05 = 7.8%, which must be too low, because early growth is above 5%. At 8%, the value is $140.07 (above the price); at 8.5%, $119.92 → IRR ≈ 8.08%.

NOTE

The IRR is the market-implied expected return; feeding it back into the DDM as r is circular. If the price differs from value, the expected return also includes the price converging to value.

Sustainable Growth ​

g=b×ROE

The sustainable growth rate is the growth that retained earnings alone can fund, with a constant capital structure and no new equity. Strictly, use the return on new investment and beginning equity. The PRAT model splits ROE with DuPont:

g=NI−DNI⏟R×NISales⏟P×SalesTA⏟A×TAEquity⏟T

Profit margin × asset turnover (P × A) = ROA, from operations; retention and leverage (R, T) reflect financial policy.

Examples.

  • ROE 25%, b 60% → g = 15%: equity $1,000,000 → $1,150,000; NI 250,000 → 287,500; dividends 100,000 → 115,000 (+15%).
  • Baggai vs Mondale, both with ROA 10%: Baggai (b 0.30, leverage 1.25) → g = 3.75%; Mondale (b 2/3, leverage 2.00) → g = 13.33% (more retention and leverage).
  • Mature company: payout 25%, ROE = r = 9% → g = 0.75 × 9% = 6.75%.
  • IBM: its 2018 ROE of 52% was driven by leverage of 7.35. Using a benchmark margin of 10.5%, turnover of 0.62 and forecast leverage of 2.0 gives ROE = 13.0%; with a 50% payout, g = 6.5%.

To estimate a mature-phase ROE: a DuPont forecast, ROE = r (no excess returns at maturity), or the median industry ROE.

Exam traps ​

  • g=b× ROE uses the retention ratio, not the payout.
  • An abnormally high historical ROE (often driven by leverage) won't last: forecast mature components.
  • Raising dividends faster than the sustainable growth rate needs external financing.

Textbook typo. The L'Oréal solution prints the GGM value as 3.85(1.0425)/(0.05 − 0.0425); the result €297.31 only works with r = 0.056 (4.0136/0.0135 = 297.3).
