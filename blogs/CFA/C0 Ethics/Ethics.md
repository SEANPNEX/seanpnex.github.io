# Ethics


## Standard I Professionalism
### Standard I(A) Knowledge of the Law

> Members and Candidates must understand and comply with all applicable laws, rules, and regulations (including the CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct) of any government, regulatory organization, licensing agency, or professional association governing their professional activities. In the event of conflict, Members and Candidates must comply with the more strict law, rule, or regulation. Members and Candidates must not knowingly participate or assist in and must dissociate from any violation of such laws, rules, or regulations.

Three duties packed into one Standard: **know**, **comply with the stricter rule**, and **do not knowingly assist — dissociate**.

#### What you must know (and what you need not)

- Understand the laws and regulations that **directly govern** your professional activities and that protect clients.
- You are **not** required to become a compliance expert or to know every law that could conceivably touch your work.
- Stay current when rules change — new products, new channels (social media, apps), and new local/regional/global requirements all count.
- When unsure, use the firm’s compliance channels; seeking counsel is recommended, but **reliance on counsel does not absolve** you if the conduct still violates law or the Code and Standards.

#### Stricter of applicable law vs Code and Standards

“Applicable law” is the law that governs the member’s or candidate’s conduct in the facts of the case. “More strict” means the rule that imposes **greater restrictions** or requires **more protective action** for investors.

| Situation | Follow |
| --- | --- |
| Applicable law **stricter** than Code and Standards | Applicable law |
| Applicable law **less strict**, or silent / no securities law | Code and Standards |
| Conduct is legal under local law but violates the Code and Standards | Code and Standards (do not do it) |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Applicable law is a **floor**, not a ceiling. If local law allows something the Code and Standards forbid (e.g. undisclosed referral fees), you still follow the Code and Standards.

Quick map for multi-jurisdiction cases (NS = no securities law; LS = less strict than Code/Standards; MS = more strict):

| Where you operate / which law applies | Duty |
| --- | --- |
| Applicable law is LS or NS | Adhere to Code and Standards |
| Applicable law is MS | Adhere to that stricter law |
| Home law says “law of the locality where business is conducted governs,” and locality is LS/NS | Code and Standards |
| Home law says locality governs, and locality is MS | That stricter local law |
| Choice-of-law points to the **client’s home country** | Whichever is stricter between that country’s law and the Code and Standards — usually: follow the stricter of the two |

#### Knowingly participate / assist — and dissociation

- You are responsible for violations you **knowingly** participate in or assist. The Standard also reaches conduct you **know or should know** may contribute to a violation.
- If you have reasonable grounds to believe client or employer activity is illegal or unethical: **dissociate**.
- Escalation path when direct talk fails:
  1. Bring it to a supervisor or compliance.
  2. Step away from the activity (remove your name from reports, refuse the assignment, stop using the misleading material, decline or drop the client).
  3. In extreme cases, resign.
- **Inaction while staying associated** can itself be treated as participation or assistance.
- Reporting fellow members/candidates to CFA Institute is **encouraged**; reporting to regulators is required only when **mandatory** under applicable law (otherwise it is discretionary whistleblowing — get legal/compliance advice).

> [!WARNING]
> Using inflated composite performance in solicitations, continuing an underwriting after discovering a misleading prospectus, or staying silent on pay-for-flow disguised as “research” payments are classic assist/dissociate traps — even if you did not create the problem.

#### Cross-border products and distribution

If you create or maintain products or packages, understand law in both **origination** and **expected sale** jurisdictions. Make reasonable efforts to check that distributors also respect distribution-country rules. When multi-jurisdiction complexity creates doubt, seek compliance or outside counsel.

#### Recommended procedures (exam-useful checklist)

**Individuals**

- Stay informed (memos, CLE / continuing education).
- Review written compliance procedures regularly.
- Keep current reference files of key statutes and rules.
- Document dissociation steps; urge the firm to stop the conduct.
- Know distribution-area laws for products you help build or sell.

**Firms (encourage)**

- Adopt a code of ethics (e.g. Asset Manager Code as a template).
- Centralize applicable-law information for staff.
- Written protocols for reporting suspected violations.

#### Exam traps

- “Legal counsel said it would be hard to prove” ≠ permission to ignore the violation.
- Home-country charterholder status does **not** override a **stricter** host-country rule (e.g. local ban on personal IPO participation).
- Minimal local insider-trading law does **not** relax Standard II(A) — follow the stricter Code/Standards rule.
- New media (Facebook, X, IM) still need current regulatory compliance; ignorance of new guidance is still a Knowledge-of-the-Law failure.


### Standard I(B) Independence and Objectivity

> Members and Candidates must use reasonable care and judgment to achieve and maintain independence and objectivity in their professional activities. Members and Candidates must not offer, solicit, or accept any gift, benefit, compensation, or consideration that reasonably could be expected to compromise their own or another’s independence and objectivity.

Clients must get work and opinions **unaffected by conflicts or pressure**. Avoid both actual loss of independence/objectivity and situations **perceived** to cause that loss. Recommendations must state your **true** opinion in **clear, unambiguous** language — no soft-pedaling to please bankers, issuers, or sales.

#### Gifts, benefits, and influence

| Source | Rule of thumb |
| --- | --- |
| **Third parties** seeking influence (issuers, brokers, underwriters, rating sponsors, managers pitching for mandates) | Modest / token gifts and ordinary business entertainment may be OK; **lavish** gifts, entertainment, IPO allocations for your personal account, or anything that could reasonably compromise judgment → reject. Best practice: reject anything that could threaten independence. |
| **Clients** (already in a compensation relationship) | Gifts / “bonuses” are treated as **supplementary compensation**. Disclose to employer **before** acceptance when possible; otherwise disclose promptly after. Disclosure lets the firm monitor favoritism vs other clients. |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Personal oversubscribed IPO allocations** to managers are prohibited under I(B) — quick profits not available to clients.

Offering **or** soliciting compromising gifts violates I(B), including gifts that do not benefit you personally (e.g. donations to a charity or political campaign tied to a hiring decision). Both sides of manager selection / procurement are covered: do not buy influence; do not sell it.

#### Pressure points (where objectivity breaks)

- **Buy-side clients** pressuring sell-side analysts against downgrades; retaliatory practices (reporting the analyst to the issuer) are improper.
- **Investment banking** pressure for favorable research on banking clients/prospects → firewalls, separate reporting lines, no IB veto over research, compensation **not** tied directly to banking deals; disclosures prominent and specific.
- **Issuer / corporate access** retaliation fears (cut off from calls/management) do not justify biased ratings.
- **Credit rating agencies** — fee and structuring conflicts; users of ratings should be mindful and may need to validate independently.
- **Performance measurement** — refuse requests to drop weak accounts from composites or to hide mandate drift.
- **Issuer-paid research** — allowed only with true independence, full conflict disclosure, diligence; compensation must be a **flat fee not linked to conclusions** (no success bonus, warrants, or equity kickers).
- **Travel funding** — best practice: commercial travel paid by you or your firm. Modest issuer-arranged travel only when commercial options are unavailable (e.g. remote property/mine tour). Sponsored “fact-finding” trips paid by a competing manager you later hire are a perception and conflict hazard.

#### Recommended procedures

- Policy that research on corporate clients reflects the analyst’s **unbiased** opinion; compensation systems that protect research integrity.
- **Restricted list** if the firm will not allow adverse opinions — disseminate facts only.
- Pay your own commercial transport and hotels for issuer visits; do not let one issuer always host.
- Token gift limits (per gift and/or annual aggregate, calibrated to local custom); ordinary entertainment OK if not meant to influence.
- Prior approval / disclosure for employee IPO participation; tight limits on private placements; supervise personal trading.
- Written independence policy: research must not report to banking or other compromising departments.
- Senior officer accountable for ethics/compliance; clear internal reporting channels.

#### Decision rules for vignettes

1. Ask: could this gift, fee, trip, or bonus **reasonably** be expected to compromise my judgment — or look that way to a client?
2. Third-party inducement vs disclosed client gift — different treatment; disclosure is mandatory for client gifts.
3. Flat fee for issuer-paid work → possible; fee tied to “attracting investors” or positive tone → violation.
4. Intrafirm pressure (sales inventory, banking deal, boss’s prior “buy”) → still issue **your** independent conclusion, or refuse the constrained assignment; consider a restricted list.
5. Hiring / “pay-to-play” — political contributions or lavish entertainment aimed at winning mandates compromise the other party’s objectivity and violate I(B) for the offeror.

> [!TIP]
> Perception matters. Even when a modest business-only site visit paid by the issuer may not violate I(B), firm policies that always self-pay are safer. For manager due-diligence trips, your employer or the plan should pay base travel/lodging.

> [!WARNING]
> Service-fee / trailer arrangements that lock you into pushing one product family after disclosure failures (or after underperformance, because you need the fee) are independence failures — often paired with VI(A) conflict disclosure issues.

### Standard I(C) Misrepresentation

> Members and Candidates must not knowingly make any misrepresentations relating to investment analysis, recommendations, actions, or other professional activities.

A **misrepresentation** is any untrue statement, omission of a fact, or other false or misleading statement. “Knowingly” covers what you know **or should have known** — including omissions that could alter an investment decision. Trust in the profession rests on accurate statements; false or misleading claims harm investors and market integrity.

#### Scope (what you must not misrepresent)

| Area | Rule of thumb |
| --- | --- |
| **Practice / credentials** | Qualifications, firm capabilities, services, performance record, investment characteristics — all must be accurate. |
| **Guarantees** | Do **not** guarantee a specific return or capital preservation on volatile investments. Built-in product guarantees (or institutional loss coverage) may be described accurately; explain limits (e.g. FDIC-insured CDs within insured amounts). |
| **Third-party material** | Credit ratings, research, testimonials, marketing — if you use them in your business, **you** share responsibility for accuracy. Verify. |
| **External managers** | Disclose intended use; do **not** present their practices as your own. |
| **Benchmarks / composites** | Use comparable benchmarks; disclose why a non-standard reference index is used. No cherry-picking accounts into composites. Do not “shop” illiquid valuations solely for higher marks. |
| **Models / omissions** | Do not knowingly omit model inputs that mislead; do not present model output as fact. |
| **Social media** | Same honesty rules as traditional client communications; perceived anonymity is not a shield. |
| **Webpages** | Keep current; protect integrity/security; full disclosure, no misleading content. |

#### Plagiarism

Copying or using others’ material in substantially the same form **without acknowledgment** violates I(C) — written **and** oral. Classic traps:

- Rebranding another firm’s report as your own
- Near-verbatim excerpts without attribution
- “Leading analysts say…” without naming sources
- Using others’ forecasts/stats while dropping their caveats
- Charts/graphs or proprietary models/spreadsheets without source/authorization
- Combining undocumented ideas/forecasts to look original

**OK:** Firm may continue using research/models created by analysts who left (firm property); no need to attribute departed authors. You may **not** reissue an old report solely under your new personal name. Third-party research may be distributed if you do **not** claim authorship — disclose the source so clients know whose expertise they are buying.

#### Recommended procedures

- Know the firm’s real capabilities; use firm-approved service/qualification language; know who may speak for the firm.
- Keep a personal qualification/experience summary.
- Verify third-party capabilities before using their materials.
- Monitor webpages for currency and security.
- **Plagiarism hygiene:** keep source copies; attribute quotations; attribute paraphrases/summaries.

#### Exam traps

- Unintentional typo discovered later → correct and stop distribution; **not** an I(C) violation if not knowing when made — but known false credentials used repeatedly → violation.
- “Guaranteed by the government” for IO strips or similar when only the underlying mortgages (not the interest stream or principal economics) are guaranteed → classic misrepresentation.
- Repackaging vendor research as “our work” → misrepresents extent of your work.
- Citing only a newspaper that relayed a study → cite the **actual** source (or both if you rely on the intermediary’s interpretation).

#### Application cases

**Example 4 — Plagiarism (Cindy Grant).** Grant specializes in Canadian mining. She reads Barton’s extensive Jefferson Mining report, obtains new drilling data by phone, adds it to Barton’s analysis, and circulates the package as **her** report.  
**Takeaway:** Reproducing large parts of another analyst’s work without acknowledgment is plagiarism — even if you add a new data point.

**Example 5 — Misrepresenting an investment (Ricki Marks).** Marks sells interest-only (IO) mortgage strips to public pension clients as “guaranteed by the US government.” Clients need government-guaranteed securities under policy/law. Only the underlying mortgages are guaranteed — not the IO interest stream or the investor’s economic outcome; rate declines and prepayments can destroy value.  
**Takeaway:** Accurate labels on complex products matter. Stretching “guaranteed” beyond what is actually guaranteed is an I(C) violation.

### Standard I(D) Misconduct

> Members and Candidates must not engage in any professional conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, or deceit or commit any act that reflects adversely on their professional reputation, integrity, or competence.

I(A) is about **applicable law** governing professional activity. I(D) is broader: any conduct that reflects poorly on **professional** integrity, reputation, or competence — lying, cheating, stealing, or other dishonest acts that adversely affect professional activities. The Code and Standards primarily target **professional-life** conduct (CFA Institute discourages unethical personal behavior generally, but I(D) is not a catch-all for every private misdeed).

#### What counts (and what usually does not)

| Conduct | I(D)? |
| --- | --- |
| Dishonesty, fraud, deceit tied to work or that damages professional trustworthiness | Yes |
| Behavior (even if not illegal) that impairs ability to perform duties (e.g. intoxication while making investment decisions) | Yes |
| Skipping due diligence and relying on others so clients’ trust is abused | Can be (absence of appropriate effort) |
| Personal bankruptcy **without** fraudulent/deceitful business conduct | Generally no |
| Civil disobedience / protest arrests that do not reflect on professional reputation, integrity, or competence | Generally no |
| Using CFA Institute enforcement to settle personal/political scores unrelated to professional ethics | Misuse; not a proper I(D) path |

#### Recommended procedures (firms — encourage)

- Firm code of ethics: personal behavior that reflects poorly on the individual, firm, or industry will not be tolerated.
- List of potential violations and sanctions (up to dismissal).
- Reference checks so hires are of good character and not barred from the industry.

#### Exam traps

- Workplace fraud (e.g. falsified insurance claims) → I(D) even if “not about securities.”
- Charity/volunteer role used for kickbacks → still fraud/deceit under I(D).
- Seeing likely misreporting and being told to “stay away” → escalate with proof per firm policy; consider regulator if internal channels fail (also IV(A)/IV(C)).

#### Application cases

**Example 1 — Professionalism and competence (Simon Sasserman).** A trust officer lunches daily with heavy drinking; clients see it; after lunch he is intoxicated while making investment decisions. Colleagues only deal with him in the morning.  
**Takeaway:** Alcohol abuse that impairs professional judgment and raises competence doubts violates I(D) — it reflects poorly on him, the employer, and the industry.

**Example 4 — Personal actions and integrity (Carmen Garcia).** An SRI fund manager / environmental activist is repeatedly arrested for nonviolent trespass at a petrochemical plant.  
**Takeaway:** Civil disobedience tied to personal beliefs generally **does not** violate I(D) when it does not reflect poorly on professional reputation, integrity, or competence.

### Standard I(E) Competence

> Members and Candidates must act with and maintain the competence necessary to fulfill their professional responsibilities.

Supports the Code duties to act with competence and diligence and to **maintain and improve** professional competence. Competence = sufficient **knowledge, skills, and abilities** for **that specific role** — not a single exam score or degree. What “competent” looks like depends on facts and duties; roles change, so the duty is to **achieve and maintain** competence over time.

| Component | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Knowledge** | Body of information applied to the function — and how effectively it is applied |
| **Skills** | Role-specific capabilities to complete tasks and goals |
| **Abilities** | Capabilities and attitudes that produce observable professional outcomes |

#### Key rules of thumb

- **Bad outcome ≠ incompetence.** A competent manager can lose money; a competent analyst can miss a forecast after thorough work. Hidden fraud that misled the whole market may not make a diligent recommender “incompetent.”
- **Education ≠ competence for a new domain.** A strong auditor may not be ready for GIPS compliance reviews; a general consultant may lack skills for a sustainable-manager search.
- **License/pass ≠ enough.** Passing a sales licensing exam without learning the fairness rules you will apply still fails I(E).
- **New role / new product:** get up to speed **before** acting (supervision skills, ESG, crypto, etc.). Do not invest client money in products you do not understand to chase a trend or retain clients.
- **CE is expected in spirit, not a mandated program.** Conferences, certifications, employer training, self-study, expert groups — any path that keeps you proficient.

#### Recommended procedures

- Ongoing professional development / CE; certifications where useful.
- Conferences, seminars, webinars; employer training; disciplined self-study.
- Expert groups; deliberately build new skills when responsibilities change.

#### Exam traps

- Promoting someone into research director / supervisor without building **management and compliance** competence for the new seat.
- Adding ESG (or any new lens) to reports **after** hiring expertise and training the team → compliant path; bolting it on with no knowledge → not.
- Rushing into unfamiliar products (e.g. crypto) on social-media hype without understanding nature or suitability → I(E) violation (often also suitability / diligence issues).

#### Application cases

**Example 6 — Understanding new investment products (Alex Halsey).** Clients push for cryptocurrency after seeing fast gains. Halsey has only general newspaper awareness, does not understand the asset or product differences, but buys a heavily promoted crypto product for clients rather than look out of touch or lose business.  
**Takeaway:** Limited knowledge + hasty client investments in unfamiliar products = failure to act with required competence.

**Example 5 — Choosing investments (Kwame Mifune).** After thorough research (credit, financials, track record), Mifune recommends Vouchsafe life policies for suitable clients. Unknown CFO fraud later bankrupts the insurer; policies become worthless. Clients call him incompetent.  
**Takeaway:** Thorough, appropriate research that still misses industry-wide deception does **not**, by itself, prove incompetence under I(E). Outcome alone is not the test.

### Standard II(A) Material Nonpublic Information

> Members and Candidates who possess material nonpublic information that could affect the value of an investment must not act or cause others to act on the information.

Trading — or inducing others to trade — on material nonpublic information (MNPI) erodes market confidence. The ban covers equities **and** derivatives, funds, and other alternatives. Selectively “knowing” the market is perceived as rigged harms capital formation for everyone.

#### Material vs nonpublic

| Test | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Material** | Disclosure would probably affect price, **or** a reasonable investor would want it before deciding — i.e. it would significantly alter the total mix of available information. Factors: specificity, divergence from public info, nature, **reliability of source**, clarity of price impact, passage of time. |
| **Nonpublic** | Not yet disseminated / available to the market generally. Waiting for the slowest reader is unnecessary once truly disseminated — but a room of analysts or a small shareholder meeting is **not** public dissemination. |

**Often material:** earnings; M&A / tender / JV; asset quality shifts; major product/patent/regulatory outcomes; big customer/supplier changes; management or auditor changes; security events (defaults, splits, dividends, offerings); bankruptcy; significant litigation; market-moving government data; large unexecuted orders; third-party rating/recommendation changes.

**Reliability matters:** a corporate insider’s fact about a major contract → likely material; a competitor’s speculation → often not. Drug-trial facts from people running the trial → likely material; educated conjecture from unconnected experts → usually not.

**Selective disclosure:** company tips to a few analysts/investors stay nonpublic until generally available. Guidance that “interprets” filings for a favored group can also be MNPI. Legitimate due-diligence MNPI under a deal agreement (merger, underwriting, rating, offering) may be used **for that engagement** — not for trading or tipping.

#### Mosaic theory (exam emphasis)

Under the **mosaic theory**, an analyst may base recommendations on conclusions drawn from:

1. **Public** information, plus  
2. **Nonmaterial nonpublic** information  

…even if the **conclusion itself** would have been material inside information had a company insider whispered it whole. Piecing together a mosaic of public facts and small, nonmaterial private scraps (customer/supplier chatter, designer/retailer opinions, labor color, FX views, etc.) is core analytical work and **does not** violate II(A).

| Mosaic OK | Violation |
| --- | --- |
| Public filings + plant visit observations + nonmaterial color from customers/competitors → sell rating anticipating weak earnings | Acting on a specific, reliable insider tip (pending deal, next week’s strike, trial failure) before public release |
| Documented interviews and analysis supporting a rating change | Tipping others (including family) with MNPI, or trading ahead of a market-moving **unpublished** analyst recommendation you received early |
| Expert-network call that deepens industry understanding **without** confidential trial/company results | Expert (or anyone) gives material confidential results → no trading until public; you must judge materiality yourself — signed “no MNPI” forms do not absolve you |

**Document the mosaic.** Save research supporting that the conclusion came from public + nonmaterial nonpublic sources [see also V(C) Record Retention]. Documentation is your evidence if someone later claims you used MNPI.

**Analyst reports as MNPI for others:** A star analyst’s unpublished recommendation can be material to the market. The analyst may give it to **clients** (paid work under mosaic/public inputs) without making it free to the world. Recipients who are **not** clients and get it early (e.g. a TV producer) must not trade or tip.

**Social media:** Membership-gated groups are not automatically “public.” Confirm material items via true public channels (filings, press, open webpages) before treating them as public. Client/public-wide posts are fine if regulatory filings and I(A) duties are met.

#### Recommended procedures

- If you get MNPI: push for **public dissemination**; if not possible, tell only designated compliance/supervisors — **no** trading or recommendation changes; do not coax insiders into private tips.
- Firm compliance: trade review, recommendation review, documented procedures, interdepartment supervision.
- Equitable disclosure culture (small vs large brokers; buy-side vs sell-side; no blackballing negative analysts).
- **Firewalls:** control interdepartment flow (preferably via compliance/legal clearance); watch / restricted / rumor lists; document and enforce; heighten review of proprietary trading when MNPI is held; physical separation and no personnel overlap across the wall; “over the wall” only via compliance; one authority decides materiality/public status.
- Personal trading limits; restricted vs watch lists (broad restricted lists can tip the market).
- Market-making while holding MNPI: stay **passive** (contra to unsolicited customer flow). Risk-arbitrage: strongest case to **suspend** when on watch list.
- Train all employees to recognize MNPI and escalate.

#### Exam traps

- Tip chains: telling a broker who buys → both tipper and trader can violate.
- Overheard IB earnings drop with salespeople walking through → failed barrier; traders on that info violate; the person who let it leak can violate too.
- Unreliable gossip (doctor’s casual takeover chat) → often **not** material → trading after other diligence may be OK.
- “My friend is a senior exec and wouldn’t break the law” + sweet earnings whisper → still your duty to avoid trading on MNPI; profits or losses irrelevant.
- Expert networks: allowed for education; **not** for confidential trial/product results used to trade.

#### Application cases

**Example 5 — Applying the mosaic theory (Jagdish Teja).** Buy-side furniture analyst studies financials, visits operations, and talks to designers and retailers about which styles sell. No clear buy appears, but Swan Furniture’s costly new designs are out of favor while peers sell conservative lines. From that mix plus P&L work, Teja concludes next-quarter earnings will drop sharply and issues a sell; managers cut positions.  
**Takeaway:** Quarterly earnings figures are material and nonpublic — but Teja’s **conclusion**, reached from public information plus **nonmaterial** nonpublic color, is classic mosaic and **not** a II(A) violation. Document those sources.

**Example 3 — Selective disclosure (Elizabeth Levenson).** Among the 10 largest shareholders invited to meet a manufacturer’s finance director, she learns the workforce will strike next Friday and cripple operations. She wants to cut the rating from buy to sell.  
**Takeaway:** Material information shared in a small group is still **nonpublic**. Until the company disseminates it to the market generally, she must not act on it.

### Standard II(B) Market Manipulation

> Members and Candidates must not engage in practices that distort prices or artificially inflate trading volume with the intent to mislead market participants.

II(B) protects market integrity. Manipulation lowers trust, raises risk premiums, and can spill across borders as markets interconnect. New products and faster communication channels create more ways to distort prices or volume — the standard covers both **information-based** and **transaction-based** schemes. **Intent** is the key test: legitimate strategies that exploit real inefficiencies are not violations.

#### Two forms

| Form | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **Information-based** | Spreading false or misleading info (or overly optimistic “independent” analysis) to induce trading — classic **pump and dump**: talk the price up, then sell into the artificial demand. |
| **Transaction-based** | Trades you knew (or should have known) could affect pricing: fake volume/price signals that divert from a fair market, or securing a dominant position to squeeze a related derivative / underlying. |

Both can run together (rumors **plus** wash trades). Profiting personally is **not** required — misleading the market is enough.

#### What is *not* II(B)

- Trading on a real view of mispricing or inefficiency.
- Liquidity “pump-priming” (e.g. exchange launch volume deals) **if fully disclosed** so participants are not misled about true liquidity.
- Honest research with a reasonable basis, distributed fairly — even if the call is aggressive — vs. sensational, speculative reports timed to move price for a client’s short.

#### Exam traps

- “Independent” microcap promotion paid in stock/cash → II(B) + often V(A) (no basis) and VI(A) (undisclosed conflict).
- Cross-fund or multi-account wash trades to juice volume/price so you can exit → still II(B) even if “for the benefit of fund participants.”
- Exaggerating earnings to a momentum subset to force a company comment / quick pop → II(B); selective preview also risks III(B) Fair Dealing.
- False chat-room rumors to harm a colleague (no personal profit) → still II(B).
- Cooking model inputs to win a rating / sale → information manipulation under II(B).

#### Application cases

**Example 7 — Pump and dump (Steve Weinberg).** To lift Moosehead & Belfast, Weinberg posts in investor chat rooms that the railroad will expand for a large lumber-shipping contract.  
**Takeaway:** Disseminating **false** information with intent to mislead is classic **information-based** II(B).

**Example 4 — Artificial volume between funds (Rajesh Sekar).** Sekar’s equity and balanced funds are both overweight illiquid Digital Design. He trades DD back and forth between the two funds to lift volume and price so outside buyers appear, letting him cut the overweight without marking the books down — “for participants,” even with extra commissions.  
**Takeaway:** Artificial distortion of **volume and price** is **transaction-based** II(B), regardless of a claimed client benefit.

## Standard III Duties to Clients
### Standard III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care

> Members and Candidates have a duty of loyalty to their clients and must act with reasonable care and exercise prudent judgment. Members and Candidates must act for the benefit of their clients and place their clients’ interests before their employer’s or their own interests.

Client interests are **paramount**. Investment actions must be for the **sole benefit** of the client, with the same prudence, judgment, and care you would apply to your own interests in similar circumstances. **Prudence** = the care, skill, and diligence of a reasonable person acting in a like capacity — in portfolio management, following the client’s investment parameters and balancing risk and return. III(A) is a **minimum** benchmark for every member regardless of job title, local law, or culture; it does **not** make everyone a legal fiduciary, and it does **not** replace a stricter legally imposed fiduciary duty [see I(A)].

#### Identifying the actual investment client (step one)

The first step in the duty of loyalty is to determine **who the client is**. Look at your role and responsibilities — members without direct investment management duties also have “clients.”

| Role | Duty of loyalty is owed to |
| --- | --- |
| Manager of an individual’s personal assets | The individual (easily identified) |
| Manager of a **pension plan or trust** | The **beneficiaries** of the plan or trust — **not** the person or entity that hired the manager |
| Manager of a fund run to an **index or expected mandate** | Investing consistently with the **stated mandate**; decisions need not be based on any individual investor’s requirements and risk profile |
| Adviser whose client invests in a fund | The adviser alone — the fund manager does not share that client duty |
| Company executive | The firm’s **public shareholders** |
| Research for the investing public as a whole | Independence and objectivity of research surpass loyalty to a single organization |
| Execution-only broker | Best execution, within the client’s trading instructions (quantity, price, timing) — **no** fiduciary duty |
| Expert witness hired by a law firm | Independence and objectivity, like an independent research analyst; the law firm must not influence the testimony |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Pension / trust money: the plan sponsor or trustee that hired you is **not** the client — the **ultimate beneficiaries** (plan participants) are. Using plan assets to help sponsor management (e.g. fend off a takeover) or to win more business for your firm violates III(A).

**Blended roles** (execution plus advice on a limited set of options, e.g. the firm’s proprietary products only): set out the limits in the client agreement at the outset; then recommend allowable products consistent with the client’s objectives and risk tolerance, disregarding any firm or personal interest in the transaction.

Members must put client obligations first in all dealings **and** endeavor to avoid all real or potential conflicts of interest.

#### Custody, governing documents, and developing the portfolio

- **Custody** (any direct or indirect access to client funds) → heightened responsibility.
- Manage assets per the **governing documents** (trust documents, investment management agreements) — the primary determinant of your powers and duties. Acting contrary to them or to applicable law risks III(A).
- The manager usually knows more than the client, so the client is vulnerable: make sure objectives and performance expectations are **realistic and suitable** and the risks appropriate; strategies should relate to the client’s **long-term** objectives.
- Watch for firm/manager goals (selling products, executing trades) that conflict with the client’s; unavoidable conflicts → clear, factual disclosure.
- Follow client **guidelines**: some clients (charitable organizations, pension plans) restrict asset types or prohibit certain securities; others set criteria on **total portfolio risk and return**.
- Judge each investment in the context of the **total portfolio**: its place in the portfolio, risk of loss and opportunity for gain, tax implications, diversification, liquidity, cash flow, and overall return requirements of the assets you manage.

> [!Note]
> The **IPS** is the working document behind III(A): the client’s objectives, constraints, and guidelines are set there, and loyalty means acting within it (IPS content is under III(C)). A fund manager meets the duty by managing to the **fund’s** IPS / mandate (Example 9 — Identifying the Client, Miller and Spears), not to any one investor’s needs.

#### Soft commissions and client brokerage

**Brokerage commission is an asset of the client** — it must be used to benefit the client, not the manager.

| Practice | Rule of thumb |
| --- | --- |
| **Soft dollars / soft commissions** (client brokerage buys research) | Paying a higher commission than normal to buy goods/services **without corresponding benefit to the client** → violates loyalty. |
| **Directed brokerage** (client directs its brokerage to buy goods/services for the client) | Not a loyalty violation — it is the client’s asset. Still seek **best price and best execution**, get the client’s assurance the goods/services benefit the **account beneficiaries**, and disclose the client may **not** get best execution from the directed broker. |
| **Best execution** | A trading process that seeks to maximize the value of the client’s portfolio within the client’s stated objectives and constraints. Required unless directed by the client as ultimate beneficiary. |
| Directing **other** clients’ trades to a broker as a reward for referrals | Only if those accounts get best price/execution **and** the practice is disclosed to them. |

#### Proxy voting

- Proxies have **economic value** to a client; voting them in an informed, responsible way is part of loyalty and an integral part of managing investments.
- Violation risks: failing to vote, voting without considering the impact of the question, or voting **blindly with management** on **nonroutine** governance issues (e.g. a change in company capitalization).
- A **cost–benefit** analysis may show voting every proxy does not benefit the client.
- **Disclose** proxy voting policies to clients.

#### Recommended procedures

**Regular account information** (if you control client assets): an itemized statement **at least quarterly** (funds and securities held, all debits, credits, and transactions); disclose where assets are maintained and where or when they are moved; **separate** client assets from any other party’s, including your own.

**Client approval:** if unsure, ask what you would expect or demand if you were the client; if still in doubt, disclose the matter **in writing** and obtain client approval.

**Firm policies** — topics to address (and encourage the firm to address):

- Follow all applicable rules and laws, and the Code and Standards.
- Establish the client’s investment objectives (experience, risk/return objectives, financial constraints) **before** recommending or acting.
- Consider all the information: (1) client needs and circumstances, (2) the investment’s basic characteristics, (3) the total portfolio’s basic characteristics.
- **Diversify**, unless inconsistent with plan guidelines or contrary to account objectives.
- Regular reviews that holdings adhere to the governing documents.
- Deal fairly with all clients — trade allocation and dissemination policies [see III(B)].
- Disclose all actual and potential conflicts; make clients aware of **all forms of manager compensation**.
- Vote proxies: in most cases, determine who is authorized to vote and vote in the best interests of clients and ultimate beneficiaries.
- Maintain confidentiality [see III(E)]; seek best execution unless directed by the client as ultimate beneficiary.
- **Place client interests first.**

#### Exam traps

- Plan sponsor’s management asks you to buy its stock for the pension plan to defeat a takeover → beneficiaries are the client; buying an overvalued stock for that purpose violates III(A).
- Commission business steered to a high-cost broker that absorbs your firm’s **rent / overhead** → no client benefit, no best execution.
- Client commissions paying for **vacation days** tacked onto a research trip → not research; must not be paid with client assets.
- Broker gives you better prices on **personal** trades, so you route trust-account business to him → own interests ahead of clients.
- Trading to hit a firm commission minimum (**excessive trading**), even with appropriate securities → III(A).
- Excluding a **regular fee-paying family account** from a suitable IPO “to avoid the appearance of conflict” → disadvantages that client; III(A) violation (exclusion is correct only if the family account is managed **outside** the firm’s normal fee structure).
- Withholding a discovered performance-reporting error from a client threatening to leave → violation; correcting it is the compliant path.

#### Application cases

**Example 1 — Identifying the Client—Plan Participants (Julian Wiley).** First Country Bank is trustee for Miller Company’s pension plan. Miller’s managers, fighting a hostile bid from Newton, persuade Wiley to buy Miller stock for the plan, hinting at more accounts for the bank. Wiley thinks the stock is overvalued but buys anyway; the heavy purchases lift the price and Newton withdraws its bid.  
**Takeaway:** The duty is to the **plan participants and beneficiaries**. Using the plan to perpetuate management, keep the sponsor’s favor, and win business violates III(A); the takeover offer had to be judged on its own merits.

**Example 2 — Client Commission Practices (JNI).** JNI sends most pension-client trading to Thompson, Inc. — high commissions, average research and execution — because the executives of the two firms are close friends, and Thompson absorbs JNI overhead such as rent.  
**Takeaway:** Using client brokerage for services that do not benefit clients, without best price and best execution, breaches the duty of loyalty.

**Example 3 — Brokerage Arrangements (Charlotte Everett).** Client Crayton directs all its trades to Scott Company, which is lining up new clients for her. To get more referrals, Everett also routes **other** clients’ trades to Scott without their knowledge.  
**Takeaway:** New client trades may go exclusively through Scott only with best price/execution, or with a written statement from the client waiving it. Rewarding Scott with other clients’ trades needs best price/execution **and** disclosure — undisclosed routing violates III(A).

### Standard III(B) Fair Dealing

> Members and Candidates must deal fairly and objectively with all clients when providing investment analysis, making investment recommendations, taking investment action, or engaging in other professional activities.

Applies when disseminating recommendations, making material changes to prior recommendations, and taking investment action (general purchases, new issues, secondary offerings). With multiple clients, favoritism can show up in the **quality and timing** of service or the **allocation of investment opportunities**. III(B) says **fairly**, not **equally**: you cannot reach every client at exactly the same moment, and not every opportunity suits every client.

#### Fair vs equal

| Fair (required) | Not required |
| --- | --- |
| No client is discriminated against in dissemination or investment action | Reaching all clients at the identical instant |
| Every client gets a **fair opportunity** to act on each recommendation | Sending every recommendation to every client (selection by **suitability and known interest** is OK) |
| Premium service levels (paid via higher fees or more brokerage) are **disclosed** and **available to everyone** | Identical service for every fee level |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Different service levels** are allowed if they (1) do not disadvantage or negatively affect other clients, (2) are **disclosed** to clients and prospects, and (3) are **not offered selectively**. Extra service comes **after** fair dissemination, never before it.

#### Investment recommendations vs investment action

| | Investment recommendations | Investment action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who** | Members whose primary function is preparing recommendations for others to act on (inside or outside the firm) | Members whose primary function is portfolio management, using internal or external recommendations |
| **Covers** | Any opinion on buying, selling, or holding — detailed report, brief update, adding/removing from a recommended list, oral communication | Trades, block allocations, new offerings, secondary financings |
| **Duty** | Encourage an equitable dissemination system; tell clients what kind of communications they will receive | Treat all clients fairly **in light of their objectives and circumstances**; allocate per firm block-allocation policy |

A recommendation distributed to anyone **outside** the organization is a communication for general distribution.

**Disseminating changes:** the duty may be **more critical** for changed recommendations than initial ones. Material changes should reach **all current clients**, with particular care for clients known to have **acted on** or been affected by the earlier advice. A client who places an order **contrary to the current recommendation** (not knowing it changed) should be told before the order is accepted.

#### IPOs and hot issues

- Distribute new issues to **all customers for whom they are appropriate**, per firm allocation policy.
- **Oversubscribed** → prorate to all subscribers, on a **round-lot** basis (avoid odd lots).
- Oversubscribed → **forgo** sales to yourself and your immediate family to free up shares for clients.
- But family accounts managed like other clients’ accounts **should not be excluded**.
- **Hot issue** = a public offering trading at a premium in the secondary market when trading starts, because of great demand. Make **bona fide public distributions**; never withhold hot issues for your own benefit or use them as a reward or incentive.
- Disclose documented allocation procedures to clients and prospects. **Client consent never overrides the duty of fairness — a patently unfair allocation stays unfair.**

Pro rata check for a partial fill (Example 7 below): requested $3 \times 10{,}000 + 2 \times 50{,}000 = 130{,}000$, received $55{,}000$:

$$
\text{Pro rata fill} = \frac{55{,}000}{130{,}000} \approx 42.3\% \;\Rightarrow\; 10{,}000 \times 42.3\% \approx 4{,}231 < 5{,}000 \text{ minimum lot}
$$

Strict pro rata would push the small accounts below the minimum lot, so the textbook accepts an allocation that gives everyone at least the minimum lot.

#### Recommended procedures

- **Develop firm policies:** encourage compliance procedures for everyone who disseminates recommendations or takes investment action; recommend procedures if none exist; tell management about possible fair-dealing violations. Formality depends on firm size and the securities involved — even a sole proprietor with only discretionary accounts should have **formal written** procedures.
- Make initial recommendations available to all customers who indicate interest; select recipients by **suitability and known interest**, not favored status; a common practice is to communicate **simultaneously** within the firm and to customers.
- **Limit** the number of people who know a recommendation is coming.
- **Shorten** the decision-to-dissemination gap: publish a short summary first if the full report takes weeks; hold research and investment-policy committee meetings on the same day if possible; use “flash” updates.
- **Pre-dissemination guidelines:** no discussing or acting on a pending recommendation.
- **Simultaneous dissemination:** time announcements so no client group is advantaged; notify branch offices at the general announcement; where appropriate, announce employee trading restrictions until distribution is complete. Follow-ups with individual clients only **after** distribution.
- Keep a **list of clients and holdings** to notify holders of changes and treat all holders fairly in a sale.
- **Trade allocation procedures** — principles: fairness in priority and in the price allocated in block trades; timeliness and efficiency; accurate records. Practices: document and time-stamp orders, changes, and cancellations; first-in, first-out (bundling allowed for efficiency); a policy on execution prices and “partial fills”; **same price and commission** for all accounts in a block; partial fills **pro rata by order size**, not below an established minimum lot (e.g. bonds); for new issues, advance indications of interest, allocation **by client** (not by portfolio manager), and a method for calculating allocations.
- **Disclose** trade allocation procedures — disclosing an inequitable method does not relieve the obligation.
- **Systematic account review** by supervisors (document both sides when buying in one account and selling in another).
- **Disclose levels of service** (same or different fees); never offer them selectively.

#### Exam traps

- Previewing a pending recommendation at lunch with best clients before the firm-wide release → III(B).
- Commingled fund always filled first, hot issues only to the commingled fund, nondiscretionary accounts last → unfair priority; disclosing the policy would not make it fair.
- Allocating trades **after** seeing which went up, to favor a client threatening to leave → III(B); allocate before execution or systematically (e.g. pro rata) as soon as practical after.
- Repeating the official EPS number in the report but telling only big clients you expect an upside surprise → III(B).
- Posting a “buy” summary to the firm’s X followers before the report goes to all clients → selective dissemination.
- Extra trading in other accounts so a favored (family-linked) plan avoids its share of the commissions that pay for shared research → III(B).

#### Application cases

**Example 1 — Selective Disclosure (Bradley Ames).** While his buy report on a small OTC company is out for factual review, Ames tells several of his best clients at lunch about the recommendation scheduled to go to all the firm’s clients early the following week.  
**Takeaway:** Giving favored clients a week’s head start on a recommendation violates III(B).

**Example 3 — Fair Dealing and IPO Distribution (Dominic Morris).** Arena, Ltd.’s IPO (to buy an arena football franchise) looks like a hot issue; Morris has quietly negotiated options for himself. Institutions oversubscribe it, and he fills all orders — including his own — while cutting the institutional blocks.  
**Takeaway:** Take no shares yourself and **prorate** among all clients. The options must also be disclosed to the firm and clients [VI(A)].

**Example 6 — Additional Services for Select Clients (Jenpin Weng).** Weng emails a new recommendation to all clients, then calls his three largest institutional clients to discuss it in detail.  
**Takeaway:** **No violation** — dissemination to everyone came first; the extra service (they presumably pay higher fees or have more assets with him) followed. Calling them first would have violated III(B).

**Example 7 — Minimum Lot Allocations (Lynn Hampton).** Five clients want a new oversubscribed bond (three × US$10,000, two × US$50,000; minimum lot US$5,000). She receives US$55,000 and allocates US$5,000 to each small account and US$20,000 to each large one.  
**Takeaway:** **No violation** — not strictly pro rata because of the minimum lot, but every client gets at least the minimum, so each can sell efficiently later.

### Standard III(C) Suitability

> 1. When Members and Candidates are in an advisory relationship with a client, they must:
>    a. Make a reasonable inquiry into a client’s or prospective client’s investment experience, risk and return objectives, and financial constraints prior to making any investment recommendation or taking investment action and must reassess and update this information regularly.
>    b. Determine that an investment is suitable to the client’s financial situation and consistent with the client’s written objectives, mandates, and constraints before making an investment recommendation or taking investment action.
>    c. Judge the suitability of investments in the context of the client’s total portfolio.
> 2. When Members and Candidates are responsible for managing a portfolio to a specific mandate, strategy, or style, they must make only investment recommendations or take only investment actions that are consistent with the stated objectives and constraints of the portfolio.

Applies to members in an **advisory relationship**: consider the client’s needs, circumstances, and objectives before recommending or acting. Review the client’s knowledge, investing experience, and financial situation — the investment’s risk vs the client’s constraints, its effect on diversification, and whether the client has the means to bear the risk. Suitability should reflect only what a **prudent person** would undertake. A proper suitability determination does **not** guarantee the investment won’t lose value. Execution-only staff and sell-side analysts may have no opportunity to judge suitability for the ultimate client.

#### Developing the IPS

Gather at inception: financial circumstances, relevant personal data (age, occupation), attitudes toward risk, investment objectives — and put them in a **written IPS**.

| IPS component | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Client identification** | Type and nature of client; existence of separate beneficiaries; approximate share of total client assets you manage |
| **Objectives — return** | Income, growth in principal, maintenance of purchasing power |
| **Objectives — risk** | Risk tolerance (suitability, stability of values) |
| **Constraints** | Liquidity needs; expected cash flows (additions/withdrawals); investable funds (assets, liabilities, commitments); **time horizon**; **tax** considerations; **regulatory and legal** circumstances; investor preferences, prohibitions, circumstances, and **unique** needs; proxy voting responsibilities and guidance |
| **Benchmarks** | Performance measurement benchmarks |

The IPS should also describe the parties’ roles and responsibilities and the **schedule for review and evaluation**. After forming long-term capital market expectations, help develop the strategic asset allocation (in the IPS, an appendix, or a separate document).

> [!Note]
> Memory hook (mine, not the textbook’s) — objectives **R**eturn and **R**isk; constraints **TTLLU**: **T**ime horizon, **T**ax, **L**iquidity, **L**egal/regulatory, **U**nique circumstances.

#### Risk profile and updating the IPS

- **Risk tolerance** is one of the most important suitability factors; consider rapidly changing markets, and analyze and quantify strategy risk **in advance**.
- **Derivatives / synthetics:** watch inherent **leverage** and limited **liquidity** — both bear directly on suitability.
- Update the IPS **at least annually**, and **before** material changes to specific recommendations or decisions.
- Changes that matter: individuals — dependents, tax status, health, liquidity needs, risk tolerance, wealth outside the portfolio, how income covers current needs; institutions — unfunded pension liabilities, savings-plan withdrawal privileges, foundation distribution requirements.
- Suitability review works best with the client’s **full portfolio** disclosed; if information is withheld, the analysis rests on what was provided.

#### Diversification and the total portfolio

- Some reasonable diversification is the norm, especially for those with legal fiduciary responsibility.
- A high-risk investment on its own may be suitable **in the context of the whole portfolio**, or if the client’s objectives contemplate speculation.
- You are responsible for suitability only on the information and criteria **actually provided** by the client.

#### Unsolicited trade requests

Requests may reflect the client’s own biases or professional experience. If you know the trade is **unsuitable**, refrain until you have **discussed** your concerns with the client.

| Situation | Action |
| --- | --- |
| **Minimal impact** on the portfolio (small trade, limited risk change) | Educate the client on how it deviates from the IPS; follow firm policy on client approval for unsuitable trades. At minimum the client **acknowledges** the discussion and accepts the conditions that make it unsuitable. |
| **Material impact** on the portfolio | Use the opportunity to **update the IPS** |
| Client refuses to update the IPS but insists | Evaluate your services: some firms allow the trade in a new **unmanaged account**; if no alternative, decide whether to **continue** the advisory relationship |

#### Managing to an index or mandate

- Fund managers must invest **consistently with the stated mandate** (e.g. a large-cap income fund should not invest heavily in speculative small-cap or start-up companies).
- They are **not** responsible for whether the fund suits each investor — that duty belongs only to members with an **advisory** relationship.

#### Recommended procedures

- **Investment policy statement:** put each client’s needs, circumstances, and objectives into a written IPS (components above).
- **Regular updates:** review objectives and constraints periodically; regularly compare client constraints with capital market expectations (changes in either may change the asset allocation). **Annual review** is reasonable unless business reasons or a major market change dictate more often; **document** attempts at review if circumstances prevent it.
- **Suitability test policies:** encourage the firm to develop them; look beyond return potential to (1) impact on portfolio **diversification**, (2) investment risk vs the client’s **assessed risk tolerance**, (3) fit with the required **investment strategy**.

#### Exam traps

- Same aggressive allocation for two clients with similar salaries but different wealth and objectives → III(C) for the client it does not fit.
- Large inheritance → the IPS **must** be updated; ability (and possibly willingness) to take risk has changed.
- Zero-dividend stock in a **high-income** fund → mandate violation unless the mandate or fund disclosures allow it.
- Even a small private-equity allocation with a **lockup** in an IPS that requires highly liquid assets → violates III(C) **and** III(A).
- Picking a subadviser on **lowest fee** alone, without matching its strategy to clients’ risk profiles → possible III(C) (client requests to diversify internationally are a cue to update IPSs).
- High-beta trading in long-horizon pension accounts to chase a quarterly bonus → III(C).
- Putting every new client into one friend’s hedge-fund product with no IPS, turning no one away → III(C); an adviser is more than a sales agent.

#### Application cases

**Example 1 — Investment Suitability—Risk Profile (Caleb Smith).** Larry Robertson (60, large asset base, much higher risk tolerance) and Gabriel Lanai (40, wants steady low-volatility returns for his children’s education) earn similar salaries. Smith puts 20% of **both** portfolios into zero-yield, small-cap, high-tech stocks.  
**Takeaway:** Possibly appropriate for Robertson, **not** suitable for Lanai — applying one client’s strategy to another with different circumstances and objectives violates III(C).

**Example 4 — Following an Investment Mandate (Louis Perkowski).** He buys an undervalued, zero-dividend financial-services stock for the **high-income** mutual fund he manages.  
**Takeaway:** Unless it fits the mandate or the fund’s disclosed allowable investments, buying off-mandate violates III(C)(2), however attractive the stock.

**Example 5 — IPS Requirements and Limitations (Max Gubler).** The insurance subsidiary’s IPS allows highly liquid assets only (large-cap equities; AA-or-better government, supranational, and corporate bonds of five years or less). Gubler puts 4% into a seed-capital private equity fund with a three-year lockup and laddered exit, though total equity stays well below its limit.  
**Takeaway:** Lockup / illiquid assets fall outside the IPS’s liquidity requirement and accepted asset classes — a violation of **III(A) and III(C)** regardless of size. The manager should also understand the client’s business beyond the IPS text.

### Standard III(D) Performance Presentation

> When communicating investment performance information, Members and Candidates must make reasonable efforts to ensure that it is fair, accurate, and complete.

Covers **any** practice that would misrepresent a performance record — performance **presentation** or performance **measurement**, **past** or **reasonably expected** performance — for individual accounts, composites, or an analyst’s or firm’s results. It is not limited to separate-account managers: pooled funds, and research analysts promoting the accuracy of their recommendations, are covered too. Never state or imply that clients will obtain a return generated in the past.

#### Brief presentations

| Allowed | Required |
| --- | --- |
| A short performance summary | Detailed supporting information **available on request** |
| Leaving out detail for space | Best practice: say the information is **limited** |

#### Recommended procedures

- **Apply the GIPS standards** — the best way to meet III(D); encourage the firm to comply.
- **Without GIPS:**
  - consider the knowledge and sophistication of the audience;
  - present a **weighted composite** of similar portfolios, not a single representative account;
  - include **terminated accounts**, showing when they were terminated;
  - disclose fully: **simulated** (model) results, a **prior entity’s** record, **gross / net of fees** or **after tax**;
  - keep the data and records used to calculate performance.

#### Exam traps

- Claiming GIPS compliance when composites are not asset weighted → III(D) for the **false claim**, even with no misuse of the data.
- A track record from a **prior firm** is allowed only with disclosure of where it was earned and your specific role.
- Showing whichever of time-weighted or money-weighted return is higher, unlabelled → III(D).
- Changing the attribution method or the month-end date to flatter results without telling clients → III(D).
- “You can expect steady 25% a year” → III(D) **and** I(C) (a guarantee).

#### Application cases

**Example 1 — Performance Calculation and Length of Time (Kyle Taylor).** Taylor’s brochure promises “steady 25% annual compound growth” because the common trust fund rose 25% last year, in line with the market. It never averaged that for more than one year, and all trust accounts averaged 5% over five years.  
**Takeaway:** Violates III(D) — he should have said the 25% was a single year and included all account categories. The promise also violates **I(C)**.

**Example 2 — Performance Calculation and Asset Weighting (Anna Judd).** Judd circulates a performance report claiming GIPS compliance, but the composites are not asset weighted.  
**Takeaway:** The violation is the **false claim of GIPS compliance** — claiming GIPS means meeting all its requirements and disclosures.

**Example 3 — Performance Presentation and Prior Fund/Employer (Aaron McCoy).** Mastermind’s ad shows the six-year equity record McCoy earned at G&P Financial without saying it was earned at G&P.  
**Takeaway:** Violates III(D). A prior-firm record may be shown only with full disclosure of where it was earned and his role.

**Example 4 — Performance Presentation and Simulated Results (Jed Davis).** Davis back-tests his fund-selection model on 1996–2003 data and advertises those results without saying they are simulated.  
**Takeaway:** Simulated results must be clearly identified, with their source disclosed — otherwise III(D).

### Standard III(E) Preservation of Confidentiality

> Members and Candidates must keep information about current, former, and prospective clients confidential unless:
> 1. The information concerns illegal activities on the part of the client;
> 2. Disclosure is required by law; or
> 3. The client or prospective client permits disclosure of the information.

Applies when you receive information (1) because of your **special ability** to conduct a portion of the client’s business or personal affairs and (2) that arises from or is relevant to that portion of the business. If the law requires disclosure or the information concerns illegal client activity, you may have an obligation to report it to the authorities.

#### Guidance highlights

| Highlight | Key point |
| --- | --- |
| **Status of client** | Covers **former** clients too — confidentiality continues after the relationship ends; follow an express authorization to disclose |
| **Compliance with laws** | Law requires disclosure → disclose. Law requires confidentiality, **even about illegal activity** → do not disclose. Inter-departmental restrictions can limit flows inside the firm. In doubt → compliance or legal counsel |
| **Electronic information and security** | Watch for accidental disclosure; no need to be an IT expert, but know the employer’s policies; encourage regular training for **all** staff, incl. receptionists and non-investment staff |
| **CFA Institute PCP** | Giving client information to the Professional Conduct Program is **not** a violation; treat the PCP as an extension of yourself |

#### Recommended procedures

- Simplest and safest: disclose client information only to **authorized fellow employees also working for that client**.
- Before disclosing information outside the confidential scope (and not illegal), ask: in what **context** was it disclosed, and is it relevant to the work? Is it background that would **improve service** to the client?
- Follow the firm’s electronic communication and storage procedures; encourage them if none exist.
- **Communicating with clients:** make sure firm-supported channels prevent accidental distribution, review privacy protections regularly, and tell clients which methods are appropriate for confidential information.

#### Exam traps

- Good intentions (helping a charity raise money) do not excuse disclosure → III(E).
- Suspected illegal activity ≠ go public at once: supervisor → remedy with the client → legal counsel; disclosure (and resignation) may follow.
- A client who discloses his own details on a properly warned social media group → **not** your violation; remove it promptly.

#### Application cases

**Example 1 — Possessing Confidential Information (Sarah Connor).** The City Medical Center trustees give Connor internal reports on renovation and expansion needs. Local businessman Thomas Kasey asks her about the building plans before deciding where to donate.  
**Takeaway:** The information is confidential and within the scope of the relationship — she must refuse.

**Example 2 — Disclosing Confidential Information (Lynn Moody).** A client tells Moody she plans to give about US$50,000 to charity for tax reasons; Moody, treasurer of the Home for Indigent Widows, tells HIW to ask the client for that amount.  
**Takeaway:** Violates III(E), however well intended.

**Example 3 — Disclosing Possible Illegal Activity (Casey Samuel).** Government officials ask to see Garcia’s pension records; Samuel already knows of possibly excessive, improper charges to the plan, and counsel says Garcia probably broke tax and fiduciary laws.  
**Takeaway:** Tell her supervisor and try to remedy it with Garcia; if that fails, get legal advice. She may have a duty to disclose and to resign.

**Example 5 — Accidental Disclosure of Confidential Information (Lynn Moody).** Moody runs a compliance-approved client group page, warning that posts are public. A client posts highly personal details; she has it removed.  
**Takeaway:** **No violation** — she took reasonable steps and acted promptly; the case points to more training and warnings.

## Standard IV Duties to Employers
### Standard IV(A) Loyalty

> In matters related to their employment, Members and Candidates must act for the benefit of their employer and not deprive their employer of the advantage of their skills and abilities, divulge confidential information, or otherwise cause harm to their employer.

Protect the firm: no conduct that would injure it, deprive it of profit, or deprive it of your skills and ability, and follow its policies as long as they do not conflict with the law or the Code and Standards. Clients always come **before** the employer. IV(A) is **not** a blanket rule to put the employer ahead of important personal and family obligations — discuss the balance with the employer when personal matters regularly interfere.

#### Employer responsibilities and independent practice

- Employers are **not** bound by the Code and Standards but should not adopt conflicting policies; senior management should avoid incentives that encourage unethical behavior. Giving your employer a copy of the Code and Standards is encouraged.
- **Independent practice** for compensation is allowed only if you notify the employer of the **type of services**, **expected duration**, and **compensation**, and receive **consent to all terms** before rendering services.
- “Practice” = any service the employer currently offers for pay. **Preparing** to compete ≠ **undertaking** independent practice.

#### Leaving an employer

| Permissible | Violation (especially in combination) |
| --- | --- |
| Preparations for a competing business, on your own time | Misappropriating **trade secrets** |
| After leaving: using skills, experience, and simple knowledge of client names | Misusing **confidential information** |
| After leaving: contacting former clients using **public information**, absent a noncompete | **Soliciting** the employer’s clients before employment ends |
| Taking what an industry agreement allows (e.g. the US Protocol for Broker Recruiting), following its procedures | **Self-dealing**; misappropriating **clients or client lists** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Until the resignation is effective, act in the employer’s best interest. Do not take records or files — **even ones you created** — without the employer’s **written** permission; erase or return firm records kept at home or on devices.

#### Social media, whistleblowing, nature of employment

- **Social media:** follow firm policy, especially when leaving. Firm-approved business accounts are **firm property**. Best practice: separate personal and professional accounts.
- **Whistleblowing:** acting against the employer (contradicting instructions, copying records) can be justified **only** to protect clients or market integrity — never for personal gain.
- **Nature of employment:** employee vs independent contractor depends on the employer’s **degree of control** (hours, location, facilities, expenses, other employers or clients). A contractor’s duties follow the agreement.

#### Recommended procedures

- **Competition policy:** know any limits on outside services; make sure a noncompete is clear before signing.
- **Termination policy:** resignation process, how clients and staff are told, social media updates, transfer of research and accounts, what client information may be taken.
- **Incident-reporting procedures:** know the whistleblowing policy; encourage confidential, anonymous reporting.
- **Employee classification:** part time, full time, contractor, and which policies apply to each.

#### Exam traps

- Taking records you **built yourself** (models, spreadsheets) → still IV(A).
- An **unpaid intern** is an employee (compensated in experience and knowledge).
- Registering a competing firm on your own time, without soliciting → preparation, **not** a violation.
- Answering an RFP your current employer is also bidding on, before you leave → IV(A) unless both the employer and the RFP issuer agree.
- Posting your firm’s password-protected research on a public personal blog → IV(A).

> [!Note]
> Memory hook (mine, not the textbook’s) — **prepare, don’t compete; leave with your head, not your files.**

#### Application cases

**Example 1 — Soliciting Former Clients (Samuel Magee).** Before resigning from Trust Assets, Magee asks four big accounts and several prospects to move to Fiduciary Management.  
**Takeaway:** Soliciting current clients and prospects while still employed violates IV(A).

**Example 2 — Former Employer’s Documents and Files (James Hightower).** Leaving Jason Investment to start his own firm, Hightower plans to copy the client list, account statements, marketing presentations, the recommended list, allocation and stock-selection models, and his own analyst spreadsheets.  
**Takeaway:** All are employer property — taking any of them without consent, even what he prepared, violates IV(A).

**Example 5 — Ownership of Completed Prior Work (Emma Madeline).** An unpaid intern at Murdoch and Lowell copies the GIPS software she helped build before joining McMillan.  
**Takeaway:** She is an employee (paid in experience) who used firm resources — copying the software violates IV(A).

**Example 10 — Soliciting Former Clients (Shawna McQuillen).** With no noncompete, she takes no records, clears her computer, and finds former clients’ phone numbers in public records after leaving.  
**Takeaway:** **No violation** — public information, no agreement, no former employer’s records.

### Standard IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements

> Members and Candidates must not accept gifts, benefits, compensation, or consideration that competes with or might reasonably be expected to create a conflict of interest with their employer’s interest unless they obtain written consent from all parties involved.

Get the employer’s permission **before** accepting compensation or other benefits from third parties for services rendered to the employer, or for any services that might conflict with its interest. This covers **direct** compensation from a client and **indirect** compensation or benefits from third parties. Such arrangements can affect loyalty and objectivity; disclosure lets the employer judge your motivations and the **true cost** of your services.

#### Key rules

| Rule | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Written consent** | Any documentable form counts (e.g. a retrievable e-mail) |
| **Nonmonetary benefits** | Count too — e.g. free memberships for board service |
| **Part-time employees** | Discuss limits on competing services during hiring; IV(B) applies to the limits identified then |

#### Recommended procedures

- Make an **immediate written report** to your supervisor and compliance officer of any compensation proposed beyond what the employer pays.
- Have the **offering party confirm** the details, including client performance incentives.
- State the **terms**: nature of the compensation, approximate amount, and duration.

#### Exam traps

- A client bonus contingent on performance (a free holiday if returns ≥ 15%) → must be disclosed in writing and consented to.
- “It’s not money” is no excuse — nonmonetary board perks still need disclosure.
- Approval first, then attending a company meeting and disclosing an unplanned dinner afterward → **no** violation.

#### Application cases

**Example 1 — Notification of Client Bonus Compensation (Geoff Whitman).** Client Carol Cochran offers Whitman and his wife a week at her Monaco condo in any year her portfolio returns at least 15% before taxes; he goes without telling his employer.  
**Takeaway:** Violates IV(B) — a supplemental, contingent arrangement could favor her account over his others; he needs his employer’s written consent.

**Example 2 — Notification of Outside Compensation (Terry Jones).** Jones gets unlimited family memberships for serving on Exercise Unlimited’s board, buys its stock for suitable clients, and doesn’t disclose because he isn’t paid in money.  
**Takeaway:** Violates IV(B) — nonmonetary compensation creates the same conflict as a director’s fee.

**Example 3 — Prior Approval for Outside Compensation (Jonathan Hollis).** After his buy report, ABC Oil invites Hollis to meet the CEO and offers its company plane. He gets approval, declines the travel, attends, joins an upscale dinner, and reports everything including the dinner.  
**Takeaway:** **No violation** — prior approval, declining the travel, and full disclosure let the firm assess any effect on his objectivity.

### Standard IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors

> Members and Candidates must make reasonable efforts to ensure that anyone subject to their supervision or authority complies with applicable laws, rules, regulations, and the Code and Standards.

Anyone with employees under their control or influence — whether or not those employees are members or candidates — has supervisory responsibility and needs in-depth knowledge of the Code and Standards. What is reasonable depends on the number of people and the work they do; supervision may be **delegated**, but you must instruct the delegates on how to prevent and detect violations. At a minimum, make reasonable efforts to **prevent and detect** violations through an **effective compliance system** — and go beyond rules: regular training and incentives that reward ethical behavior.

#### System for supervision

- Procedures must be **established, documented, communicated, and followed**, then monitored and enforced. “Adequate” = meets industry standards, regulation, the Code and Standards, and the firm’s circumstances.
- They must be in place **before** a violation, aimed at the activities most likely to cause misconduct, and fit the firm’s size and nature.
- No authority over firm-wide policy? Still work with superiors on a code of ethics, compliance procedures, training, ethical incentives, and best-practice standards (GIPS, Asset Manager Code).
- Inadequate system → tell senior management and recommend fixes; if you clearly cannot supervise, **decline supervisory responsibility in writing** until reasonable procedures exist.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> On learning of a possible violation: **promptly** investigate its extent. The employee’s assurances, reporting it up the chain, or just warning the employee are **not enough**. Pending the outcome, limit or increase monitoring of the employee’s activities.

#### Supervision includes detection

| Situation | IV(C)? |
| --- | --- |
| Reasonable procedures adopted and enforced, violation still slips through | Possibly **no** violation |
| Violations keep occurring | May signal procedures are **inadequate** |
| You know or should know procedures are **not being followed** | Violation |

#### Recommended procedures

- **Code of ethics:** encourage one, kept **separate** from detailed compliance procedures, in plain language, and shared with clients.
- **Adequate compliance procedures:** a clear written manual tailored to the firm; easy to understand; a compliance officer with defined authority and resources; a supervision hierarchy; checks and balances; scope; documented monitoring and testing; permissible conduct; reporting of violations and sanctions.
- **Once in place:** disseminate, update periodically, educate continually, send reminders, include professional conduct in performance reviews, review employee actions, enforce.
- **After a violation:** respond promptly, investigate thoroughly, increase supervision or limit the wrongdoer pending the outcome, review procedures.
- **Education and training:** regular, alongside the code of ethics.
- **Incentive structure:** tie rewards to client interests and **how** outcomes are achieved, not how much is generated for the firm.

#### Exam traps

- Telling colleagues about a pending recommendation change with no procedures to stop them trading on it → the **supervisor** violates IV(C).
- Passing on a subordinate’s unreviewed recommendation (based on merger rumors) → IV(C).
- A compliance supervisor paid partly on trading commissions who ignores suspicious volume → IV(C); watch your own conflicts.
- Supervisors brushing off an employee’s concerns → IV(C); they must review them.

#### Application cases

**Example 1 — Supervising Research Activities (Jane Mattock).** Mattock orally tells H&V executives she’s changing Timber Products from buy to sell; Frampton, who reports to her, sells for himself and discretionary clients, and others tip institutional customers before publication.  
**Takeaway:** Violates IV(C) — she needed procedures to prevent and detect trading on, and spreading of, the unpublished change.

**Example 2 — Supervising Research Activities (Deion Miller).** Miller passes Georgia Ginn’s unreviewed buy memo on Excelsior, based on merger rumors, to the portfolio managers before going on vacation.  
**Takeaway:** Violates IV(C) — he didn’t check for a reasonable basis or for material nonpublic information.

**Example 3 — Supervising Trading Activities (Roberta Ann Mason).** Trainee David Edwards lets a customer pay for Highland shares from profits on their immediate resale; Mason, the compliance supervisor paid partly on trading commissions, sees the volume and does nothing.  
**Takeaway:** Violates IV(C) — supervisors must be especially alert to conflicts between their self-interest and their duties.

**Example 5 — Accepting Responsibility (Meredith Rasmussen).** The hedge fund’s reported performance ignores a sharp market break; the head of trading and the compliance officer tell Rasmussen not to ask.  
**Takeaway:** The **supervisors** violate IV(C) by dismissing her concern — they must review it and build an ethical culture. (Her whistleblowing is covered under IV(A).)
