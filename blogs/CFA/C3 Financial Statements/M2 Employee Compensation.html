Employee Compensation ​

Types of Employee Compensation ​

Accounting standards divide compensation into 5 general types, distinguished by:

  • Timing: how long after the employee's service the payment is made
  • Form of payment: cash, shares, or other benefits
TypeWhat it coversExamples
Short-term benefitsPaid within 12 months after the end of the period in which the service is renderedSalaries, annual bonuses, paid vacation, non-monetary benefits (medical care, housing)
Long-term benefitsNot expected to be paid within 12 months after the end of the service periodLong-term paid leave (e.g., sabbaticals), long-term disability benefits
Termination benefitsPaid because employment ends, not in exchange for serviceSeverance pay, outplacement support
Share-based compensationPaid in the employer's shares, or in cash based on the share priceRestricted stock, stock options, stock appreciation rights
Post-employment benefitsPaid after employment ends, in exchange for service during employmentPensions, retiree medical benefits

Terminology ​

  • Grant date: The employer and the employee agree on the terms of the award. For share-based compensation, the award's fair value is measured on this date (see Financial Reporting for Share-Based Compensation).
  • Vesting period: The period of service the employee must complete to earn the award.
  • Vesting date: The vesting conditions are met. The employee becomes unconditionally entitled to the compensation, so the employer now has an obligation.
  • Settlement date: The compensation is paid, in cash or in shares.

The division of periods is similar to the terms of an option: granted, vested, then exercised (settled).

The cost of compensation is recognized over the vesting period, as the employee renders the service that earns it, not when it is paid. Vesting is followed by settlement.

  • During the vesting period: The employer recognizes compensation expense as the compensation vests. The other side of the entry depends on how the award will be settled:
    • Cash-settled (salaries, cash bonuses, pensions, stock appreciation rights paid in cash): a liability.
    • Equity-settled (restricted stock, stock options): equity (paid-in capital; see the journal entries). No liability arises, because the employer owes shares, not cash.
  • Expense or asset: The cost follows the employee's function. Pay for staff who produce inventory or build PP&E is capitalized into that asset, and reaches the income statement later as COGS or depreciation. Pay for management and administrative staff is expensed directly in SG&A.
  • At settlement: Shares are issued, or the liability is paid in cash. For equity-settled awards, nothing more is expensed after vesting, even if the options are never exercised (see the option example).

Accounting Principles ​

CategoryShort-Term BenefitsEquity-Settled Share-Based CompensationPost-Employment Benefits
Typical vesting periodDays or weeksYearsYears or decades
Form of paymentCashSharesCash
Amount recognized over the vesting periodUndiscounted salary, wage, etc.Fair value, measured on the grant datePresent value of estimated future benefits

Reading the table:

  • The longer the wait, the more the amount relies on estimates. Short-term benefits are paid within days or weeks, so they are recognized at the undiscounted amount owed. Post-employment benefits are paid decades later, so they are measured at the present value of benefits (see Pension Obligation) that depend on assumptions about salaries, lifetimes and discount rates.
  • Equity-settled awards are fixed at the grant date. Their fair value is measured once, on the grant date, and is not remeasured for later changes in the share price. That amount is spread over the vesting period.

Regardless of the form of compensation, most companies aggregate compensation expense and report it on the income statement by the employee's function (e.g., in COGS or SG&A), rather than as a separate line.

NOTE

The exception is termination benefits. They are often incurred as part of a corporate restructuring and are reported within restructuring costs. Analysts often treat restructuring costs as non-recurring, so check whether they recur before excluding them.

Share-Based Compensation ​

Share-based compensation pays employees in the employer's shares, or in cash linked to the share price. It is common for senior management, and for early-stage companies that have little cash to spare.

Advantages and Disadvantages ​

Advantages:

  1. Aligns interests. Employees who own shares gain and lose with shareholders, which reduces principal–agent conflicts.
  2. Improves retention. A multi-year vesting period gives employees a reason to stay, because unvested awards are forfeited when they leave (see Vesting Conditions).
  3. Preserves liquidity. Equity-settled awards require no cash outflow, which matters most for start-ups and high-growth companies.

Disadvantages:

  1. Limited link to the employee's own effort. Most employees have little influence over the share price, which also moves with the market, so the award may not change how they act.
  2. Distorted risk-taking. It can push managers either way:
    • Options pay off only on the upside, so they can encourage excessive risk-taking.
    • A large holding of shares ties up the manager's own wealth in the company, so it can make them too risk-averse.
  3. Concentrated risk for employees. Employees already depend on the company for their salary. Share-based pay makes their wealth fall with the share price as well, and an underperforming share may leave them worse off than equivalent cash pay.
  4. Dilution. Issuing new shares on vesting or exercise reduces existing shareholders' ownership and EPS (see Diluted Shares Outstanding).

Vesting Conditions ​

  1. Service condition: The award vests on a future date, as long as the employee is still employed then.
  2. Performance condition: The award vests only if a performance target is met, usually in addition to a service condition. There are two kinds:
    • Market condition: tied to the share price, e.g., a target share price or total shareholder return relative to an index.
    • Non-market condition: tied to operating results, e.g., EPS growth, revenue or ROE targets.

NOTE

The two kinds of performance condition are accounted for differently:

  • A market condition is built into the grant-date fair value (a share-price target lowers it). The expense is recognized as long as the service condition is met, even if the market target is missed.
  • A non-market condition is not part of the fair value. Instead, the expense is based on the number of awards expected to vest, re-estimated each period (as in the option example). If the target is missed, the expense recognized so far is reversed.

The same true-up applies to a service condition: awards forfeited by employees who leave before vesting reverse their expense.

Example: Market vs Non-Market Condition ​

Why the market-condition expense stays. The expense measures the employee service the company received, valued at the grant date. With a market condition, the fair-value model (e.g., Monte Carlo) already prices in the chance that the share-price target is missed, so the 24m fair value is lower than it would be without the target. Missing the target is one of the outcomes that price already allowed for. Reversing the expense would count that risk twice. The share price is also outside management's control, so the standards don't let the expense follow it.

Setup. An award with a grant-date fair value of 24m vests after 3 years of service, with an expense of 24/3=8m a year. At the end of year 2, 16m has been recognized.

Outcome at the end of year 3Year 3 expenseCumulative expense
Market condition (share-price target) missed, employee still employed8m24m: nothing reversed
Non-market condition (e.g., EPS target) missed−16m (reversal)0
Employee leaves in year 3 (service condition fails)−16m (reversal)0

A non-market condition is not in the fair value, so the expense follows the number of awards expected to vest and is trued up to the actual result. A market condition gives no true-up: once the service is rendered, the full 24m is expensed whether or not the award ever pays out.

Equity-Settled Share-Based Compensation ​

Equity-settled awards are paid in shares. Their fair value is measured on the grant date and spread over the vesting period (see the timeline). It is not remeasured for later changes in the share price.

Restricted StockRestricted Stock Units (RSUs)Employee Stock Options (ESOs)
What the employee getsShares issued at grant, which cannot be sold or transferred until they vestA promise of shares, delivered at settlementThe right to buy shares at a fixed exercise price
Voting and dividends before vestingYes, usuallyNo (sometimes dividend equivalents)No
Value to the employeeFull share priceFull share priceOnly the gain above the exercise price
Grant-date fair valueShare price at grantShare price at grant (less expected dividends the holder does not receive)Option-pricing model (e.g., Black–Scholes–Merton, binomial)
  • Performance shares (PSUs) are RSUs whose number of shares depends on a performance condition.
  • Employee stock options are typically granted at the money: the exercise price equals the share price on the grant date. The option has no intrinsic value at grant, so it is worth something to the employee only if the share price rises. Its fair value is time value alone, which is why it must be estimated with an option-pricing model rather than read from the share price.

IMPORTANT

An option's grant-date fair value, and therefore the expense, depends on management's inputs: expected volatility, expected term, dividend yield and the risk-free rate. A higher volatility, longer term or higher risk-free rate raises the fair value; a higher dividend yield lowers it. Check whether the assumptions, disclosed in the notes, are reasonable: assuming a lower volatility or a shorter expected term reduces compensation expense.

Financial Reporting for Share-Based Compensation ​

  • Fair value is measured once, on the grant date. Later changes in the share price have no effect on the expense.
    • Restricted stock and RSUs: the market price of the underlying shares on the grant date. RSUs usually receive no dividends before they vest, so their price is typically adjusted downward for the dividends expected over the vesting period.
    • Options: estimated on the grant date with an option-pricing model, such as Black–Scholes–Merton (FSA does not ask for the calculation; it is covered in Derivatives).
  • The cost is recognized over the vesting period, as the employee provides the service. The amount is based on the number of awards expected to vest, re-estimated each period (see Vesting Conditions).
  • Settlement: RSUs settle when they vest. Options settle later, when (and if) the employee exercises them, at any time until expiry.

For equity-settled awards, the cost builds up in a share-based compensation reserve within equity:

Under US GAAP, windfalls and shortfalls pass through income tax expense, so the effective tax rate moves with the share price. Strip them out when forecasting the tax rate.

The IFRS row is the curriculum's simplification. Under IAS 12, only the part of the deduction above the cumulative expense goes to equity; a shortfall generally goes through profit or loss.

Diluted Shares Outstanding ​

Share-based awards are potential shares, so diluted EPS counts them with the treasury stock method: the company is assumed to issue the shares and use the proceeds to buy back shares at the average market price.

Diluted shares=Basic shares+N−Assumed proceedsP¯Assumed proceeds=N×X+Unrecognized compensation expense―
  • N: shares issued on exercise or settlement
  • X: exercise price; 0 for RSUs
  • P¯: average share price during the period
  • Unrecognized compensation expense―: the expense still to be recognized over future vesting periods, averaged over the period (the average of the two period-end values, which are disclosed in the notes). The service the company has yet to receive counts as part of what it is paid for the shares, as if it were cash used to buy back shares.

Dilutive and Anti-Dilutive ​

The awards are included only if they are dilutive, i.e., if N−Assumed proceeds/P¯>0, so that diluted EPS falls. Anti-dilutive awards are excluded (and disclosed).

  • In-the-money options may be dilutive, if the share price exceeds the exercise price plus the unrecognized expense per option.
  • At-the-money and out-of-the-money options are anti-dilutive: the assumed proceeds buy back more shares than are issued.
  • RSUs are usually dilutive, because their exercise price is zero.
  • If the company reports a net loss, all potential shares are anti-dilutive.

Example: Diluted Shares ​

JinSan has 200 million basic shares outstanding in 20X1 and reports positive net income. Its only share-based awards are:

  • 1.8 million RSUs

Financial Statement Modeling ​

Disclosures for Share-Based Compensation ​

IFRS requires disclosures so that users understand:

  1. The nature and extent of share-based payment arrangements during the period
  2. How the fair value of the equity instruments granted was measured
  3. The effect on net income and on the financial position

US GAAP requires similar disclosures. They are typically made in the notes to the financial statements, and usually include the information an analyst needs for modeling:

  • A roll-forward of awards: outstanding at the beginning of the year, granted, vested or exercised, forfeited, and outstanding at the end, with the weighted-average grant-date fair value or exercise price
  • The inputs to the option-pricing model (volatility, expected term, dividend yield, risk-free rate), which drive the grant-date fair value
  • Share-based compensation expense for the year, often split by income-statement line
  • Unrecognized compensation expense and the weighted-average period over which it will be recognized, which gives the future expense on awards already granted, and feeds the treasury stock method

The proxy statement discloses the compensation of executive management and directors: salaries, bonuses, awards granted and the performance targets attached to them. It shows how well management's incentives are aligned with shareholders'.

Modeling the Income Statement ​

Share-based compensation is typically not a separate line item on the income statement. It is included in operating expenses (COGS, SG&A, R&D) according to each employee's role (see Accounting Principles). Forecasting those lines as a percentage of revenue therefore captures share-based compensation indirectly.

The common approach to modeling it explicitly is to forecast share-based compensation as a percentage of revenue, based on history, management guidance or industry peers. The other side of the entry goes to equity (the share-based compensation reserve).

For early-stage companies, share-based compensation tends to decline as a percentage of revenue as the company matures, so a constant percentage overstates it. Model it discretely instead, for example from the unrecognized expense on existing awards (see Disclosures) plus expected new grants.

Modeling Cash Flow and the Share Count ​

  • Cash flow statement: Share-based compensation is non-cash, so it is added back to net income in CFO (indirect method). Option exercises bring in cash (CFF); RSU vesting usually involves no cash.
  • Awards outstanding: Forecast the awards with a roll-forward, consistent with the expense forecast:
    1. Grants of share-based awards, net of forfeitures: from their historical growth in the notes
    2. Settlements of awards (RSUs vesting, options exercised): e.g., as a fixed portion of the awards outstanding
Awardsend=Awardsbegin+Grants (net of forfeitures)−Settlements
  • Shares outstanding: Settlements (RSUs vested, options exercised) become new shares.
Sharesend=Sharesbegin+Settlements+Issuances−Repurchases

Diluted shares then add the awards still outstanding, using the treasury stock method (see Diluted Shares Outstanding).

Valuation Considerations ​

  1. Multiples-based valuation. Companies often report non-GAAP measures, such as adjusted EBITDA or adjusted EPS, that exclude share-based compensation. These inflate profits and make the company look cheaper. Analysts can use them, adjusting the denominator of the multiple, but must be consistent and transparent: compare the company and its peers on the same basis, GAAP with GAAP or the same non-GAAP measure throughout. Simply ignoring share-based compensation is flawed, because it is a real transfer of value from shareholders to employees, paid in shares instead of cash.

  2. DCF valuation. Free cash flow adds back share-based compensation as a non-cash charge. Left there, the DCF values the company as if its employees worked for free, while the cost really arrives as dilution. The model must account for:

    1. Dilution from awards outstanding but unvested: use diluted shares (treasury stock method) to compute value per share. A more conservative choice is basic shares plus all potentially dilutive securities.
    2. Dilution from future awards: the practical method is to deduct share-based compensation from free cash flow, i.e., treat it as a cash expense and not add it back to non-cash charges (NCC):
FCFF=NI+(NCC−SBC)+Int(1−t)−FCInv−WCInv

where SBC is share-based compensation expense.

NOTE

Deducting share-based compensation from free cash flow is not technically exact, but increasing the share count for expected future grants, or reducing the equity value, should give the same result. Use only one of these for future awards; counting them in both the cash flows and the share count double-counts the dilution.

Post-Employment Compensation ​

Post-employment benefits are paid after employment ends, in exchange for service during employment. They include cash benefits (pensions) and non-monetary benefits (retiree health care, life insurance). Plans are classified as either defined contribution (DC) or defined benefit (DB):

Defined Contribution (DC)Defined Benefit (DB)
The sponsor promisesA contribution todayA benefit at retirement
Who bears investment riskThe employeeThe sponsor
Obligation after contributingNonePay the promised benefits, whatever the plan's assets earn
ExpenseThe contribution for the periodTotal periodic pension cost (TPPC), from actuarial estimates
Balance sheetOnly contributions still unpaidFunded status: net pension liability or asset

Defined Contribution ​

In a DC plan, the sponsor makes agreed-upon contributions to the plan and has no further obligation. Employees usually choose how the money is invested, and bear the investment risk.

Financial Reporting for DC ​

  • The expense is the contribution owed for the period. Like other compensation, it is reported by the employee's function, with no discrete line item.
  • Contributions owed but not yet paid at period-end are recognized as a current liability.

Defined Benefit ​

DB plans are commitments to pay a defined amount after retirement, either as a lump sum or as periodic pension payments until death. The employer bears the investment risk: if plan assets earn less, the employer must contribute more.

A typical benefit formula is based on the final salary:

Annual benefit=Final salary×N×x%Final salary=Current salary×(1+g)T

where N is the years of service, x% the accrual rate, g the assumed salary growth and T the years to retirement. The projected benefits are discounted to the present, and the obligation builds up as the employee accumulates years of service.

  • A closed plan no longer admits new employees; existing members continue to accrue benefits.
  • A frozen plan stops accruing benefits for further service, so current members' future benefit payments are fixed.

Pension Obligation ​

The pension obligation is a liability of the employer. As part of their total compensation, employees earn pension benefits with each year of service; the value of the benefits earned in the year is the current service cost (CSC).

US GAAP measures the obligation as the present value of future benefits earned by employees for service to date, using the projected final salary: the projected benefit obligation (PBO). (IFRS calls it the defined benefit obligation.)

The PBO relies on actuarial assumptions: salary growth, retirement dates, employee turnover and mortality. The discount rate is the yield on high-quality (investment-grade) corporate bonds, or on government bonds where there is no deep market in corporate bonds.

Plan Assets ​

The sponsoring company is responsible for making contributions to the plan, either to meet regulatory minimum funding levels or on a discretionary basis, to ensure that future benefits can be paid.

Plan assets are the property of the plan, not of the sponsoring company, so once contributed they cannot be withdrawn. The plan is a separate legal entity that prepares its own financial statements; its assets do not appear on the company's balance sheet (only the net funded status does).

Funded Status ​

Funded status=Fair value of plan assets−PBO
  • An underfunded plan (deficit) is reported as a net pension liability, usually in other non-current liabilities.
  • An overfunded plan (surplus) is reported as a net pension asset, usually in other non-current assets. Under IFRS, the asset is capped by an asset ceiling: the present value of the economic benefits the company can get from the surplus, through refunds or lower future contributions. Plan assets belong to the plan, so a surplus is worth only what the company can recover.

NOTE

Different plans cannot be netted: a surplus in one plan does not offset a deficit in another.

Financial Treatments ​

Under IFRS, TPPC has 3 components, 2 recognized in P&L and 1 in OCI:

  1. Service cost: current service cost and past service cost, both recognized as an operating expense in P&L, classified by employee function. Example: 60+20=80.

  2. Net interest expense (income): the discount rate times the funded status at the start of the year, recognized in P&L. A deficit gives an expense; a surplus gives income. Example: 5%×100=5, which is interest cost 50 less interest income on plan assets 5%×900=45.

  3. Remeasurements, recognized in OCI:

    1. The difference between the actual return on plan assets and the interest income assumed in the net interest calculation. Example: 60−45=15 gain.
    2. Actuarial gains and losses. Example: 30 loss.

    Net remeasurement loss: 30−15=15.

NOTE

Under IFRS, remeasurements in OCI are never reclassified to P&L. They may be transferred directly to retained earnings within equity.

Under US GAAP, the five items are treated as follows:

  1. Current service cost: in P&L, as an operating expense. Example: 60.
  2. Past service cost: in OCI, then amortized into P&L over the employees' average remaining service period. Example: 20 in OCI, of which 2 is amortized this year.
  3. Interest cost less the expected return on plan assets: in P&L. The expected return uses the company's own expected rate of return, not the discount rate. Example: 50−7%×900=50−63=−13.
  4. The difference between the actual and expected return: in OCI, as part of the actuarial gains and losses. Example: 60−63=−3, a loss.
  5. Actuarial gains and losses: in OCI, and amortized into P&L only under the corridor approach: the net cumulative gain or loss in AOCI that exceeds 10% of the greater of the PBO and plan assets (at the start of the year) is amortized over the employees' average remaining service period. Companies may also recognize them faster. Example: 30 loss, and nothing amortized this year since AOCI started at 0.

US GAAP pension expense in P&L: 60+50−63+2=49. OCI: (20−2)+3+30=51. Under US GAAP, only the service cost is reported in operating income; the other components are presented outside it.

IMPORTANT

The same pension cost of 100 shows up as 85 in IFRS P&L but only 49 in US GAAP P&L. US GAAP's expected return (7%, chosen by management, above the 5% discount rate) and its deferral of past service cost and actuarial losses both lower reported pension expense. When comparing companies, look at the TPPC and the assumptions, not only the P&L line.

Other Post-Employment Benefits ​

Other post-employment benefits, such as retiree health care and life insurance, are accounted for much like DB pension plans: the employer's obligation is estimated with actuarial assumptions (including the expected trend in health care costs).

Unlike pensions, companies are generally not required by regulation to pre-fund these plans, so many have few or no plan assets, and the funded status is close to the full obligation.

Financial Modeling Considerations ​

Disclosures for Post-Employment Benefit Plans ​

DC plans need little disclosure: the expense for the period is the contribution, and nothing is left to estimate.

DB plans need much more. IFRS requires disclosures that:

  1. Explain the characteristics of the plans and the risks associated with them
  2. Identify and explain the amounts in the financial statements arising from the plans
  3. Describe how the plans may affect the amount, timing and uncertainty of future cash flows

US GAAP requires similar disclosures. In the notes, they usually include what an analyst needs for modeling:

  • Roll-forwards of the PBO and plan assets, which give the funded status at both ends of the year
  • The components of pension cost, and where each is reported (operating expense, finance cost, OCI)
  • The actuarial assumptions: discount rate, salary growth, life expectancy, inflation, the health care cost trend for OPEB and, under US GAAP, the expected return on plan assets
  • A sensitivity analysis of the obligation to each key assumption (required under IFRS)
  • Expected contributions for the next year and expected benefit payments for the next several years
  • The allocation of plan assets (equities, bonds, real estate and so on), which shows the investment risk the sponsor bears

Assumptions are where management has discretion. Each assumption below lowers the reported obligation or the pension expense, so a company that is at the favorable end of its peers on several of them is reporting aggressively:

AssumptionAggressive choiceEffect
Discount rateHigherLower PBO; usually lower service cost
Salary growthLowerLower PBO, service cost and interest cost
Life expectancyShorterLower PBO: benefits are paid for fewer years
InflationLowerLower PBO where benefits or salaries are indexed to inflation
Health care cost trend (OPEB)LowerLower OPEB obligation and cost
Expected return on plan assets (US GAAP only)HigherLower pension expense in P&L; no effect on the PBO or the funded status

NOTE

Compare the assumptions with peers, and with the company's own history. A change in an assumption shows up as an actuarial gain or loss in OCI, so it can move the funded status without touching P&L. Use the IFRS sensitivity analysis to restate the obligation under a peer's discount rate.

Modeling Post-Employment Benefits ​

DC plans are straightforward. The contributions are part of the cost of each employee, so they are forecast implicitly within operating expenses (COGS, SG&A, R&D), in the same way as share-based compensation.

DB plans are modeled through the components of pension cost and the cash contributions:

ItemHow to forecast itWhere it goes
Service costFrom history and the notes, e.g., growing with headcount and salaries; zero for a frozen planOperating expense, by function
Net interest expense (income)Discount rate × funded status at the start of the yearFinance cost (IFRS); outside operating income (US GAAP, which uses the expected return)
RemeasurementsUsually assumed to be zero: actuarial gains and losses and the excess return on plan assets cannot be predictedOCI
ContributionsThe expected contribution disclosed for the next year, then a funding plan or regulatory minimumCash outflow (CFO)

The funded status then rolls forward. Benefits paid drop out, because they reduce plan assets and the PBO equally (see Funded Status):

Funded statusend=Funded statusbeg−Service cost−Net interest±Remeasurements+Contributions

Example (IFRS, continuing the example above). The plan starts next year with a deficit of 130 (plan assets 990, PBO 1,120). The discount rate stays at 5%. Service cost is forecast at 64, the notes disclose an expected contribution of 75, and remeasurements are assumed to be zero.

  • Net interest expense =5%×130=6.5, a finance cost
  • Pension cost in P&L =64+6.5=70.5, of which 64 is in operating expenses
  • Funded status at the end of next year =−130−64−6.5+75=−125.5

The contribution of 75 exceeds the pension cost of 70.5, so the deficit shrinks by 4.5.

Valuation Adjustments for DB Plans ​

The same logic applies to DB pensions and OPEB:

  1. Start from the funded status. An underfunded plan is a debt-like claim: the company must pay it off with future contributions. Deduct it from enterprise value, together with debt, to get equity value. It is often deducted after tax, because contributions are usually tax-deductible. An overfunded plan is usually ignored: the surplus belongs to the plan, and the company can recover it only through refunds or lower future contributions (see the asset ceiling).
  2. Treat future service cost as a cash operating cost, as with share-based compensation: deduct it in free cash flow, and do not add it back as a non-cash charge. It is the cost of the pension benefits employees earn each year, in the same way as their salaries.
  3. Leave net interest out of FCFF. Once the deficit is counted as debt, its interest is a financing cost, like interest on debt. Including it in the cash flows as well would count the deficit twice. For the same reason, contributions above service cost are like repaying debt, not an operating outflow.
  4. Ignore remeasurements in the forecast: they are not predictable, and their effect is already in the latest funded status.

In the example, the deficit of 130 is deducted from enterprise value: 130, or 130×(1−25%)=97.5 after tax at a 25% tax rate. FCFF includes service cost of 64 as an operating cost, and leaves out the 11 of contributions above service cost (75−64): they pay the deficit's interest (6.5) and reduce the deficit itself (4.5), as debt service would.

NOTE

Do not deduct the deficit from enterprise value and model the contributions that repay it in free cash flow. That counts the same obligation twice, like the dilution warning for share-based compensation.
