# Corporate Restructuring

The types of corporate restructuring and why issuers pursue them; how an analyst evaluates one (initial evaluation, preliminary valuation, modeling and valuation); and how investments, divestments and restructurings change EPS, net debt to EBITDA and WACC. Most of the module is worked cases.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Synergies** | A combination worth more than the sum of its parts: **cost** synergies (economies of scale) or **revenue** synergies (economies of scope, cross-selling, pricing power) |
| **Conglomerate discount** | An issuer valued below the sum of its parts |
| **Pro forma financial statements** | Estimated statements that include the effect of the restructuring |
| **Takeover (control) premium** | % by which the deal price per share exceeds the unaffected price |
| **Cap rate** | Net operating income ÷ property value: the reciprocal of a valuation multiple |

## Why Companies Restructure

Companies move through a life cycle: start-up → growth → maturity → decline. Revenue growth slows and free cash flow peaks at maturity; debt rises from about 0% at start-up to 20% or more at maturity. Managers act to change that path, in three ways:

* **Investment**: increase the company's size or scope, and so its revenue and perhaps its growth. Here this means **external (inorganic)** growth, not capex or R&D.
* **Divestment**: reduce size or scope, typically shedding slower-growing, less profitable or riskier operations.
* **Restructuring**: improve the cost and financing structure to raise growth, improve profitability or cut risk, **without** changing size or scope.

### Issuer-Specific Motives

| Action | Motives |
| --- | --- |
| **Investment** | **Synergies**: cost (one head office; better capacity use and route density; R&D; sales and marketing) and revenue (cross-selling, e.g., bank + insurer; bargaining power from less competition). **Growth.** **Capabilities or resources**: vertical integration lowers costs and risk; cross-border deals bring new markets, facilities and talent. **Undervalued targets** |
| **Divestment** | **Focus** on core operations (e.g., Daimler's 2021 spin off of Daimler Truck). **Valuation**: a conglomerate discount, from diseconomies of scale or scope, incompatible businesses, or markets overlooking a business; a unit may be worth more on its own (Novartis spun off Alcon in 2019 at over 30× EPS, about twice Novartis's multiple; Alcon rose 35% in two years while Novartis was flat). **Liquidity**: sell businesses to cut unsustainable leverage (often at low prices, good for the buyer). **Regulation**: antitrust conditions on a deal, or court-ordered divestiture |
| **Restructuring** | **Improve returns on capital**, either opportunistically (change the business model, trim costs, change the balance sheet) or because forced (returns below the required return, from weak management, falling demand, competition or overcapacity). **Financial distress**, including bankruptcy and liquidation |

### Top-Down Motives

* **High security prices**: all types of restructuring are **pro-cyclical** (BCG: a 0.80 correlation between the MSCI World and deal volume, 2000–2019), because of greater CEO confidence; cheaper financing (lower rates and ERP mean less interest and less dilution); and managers who think their stock is **overvalued** use it as currency for acquisitions, sales and spin offs.
* **Industry shocks**: regulatory changes, technological changes and changes in industry growth cause industry-specific waves.

> [!NOTE]
> Deals done in **weak** economies have created more value on average (BCG: nearly 10% higher three-year shareholder return than deals in strong economies).

## Types of Corporate Restructuring

| Investment (grow) | Divestment (shrink) | Restructuring (improve) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equity investment | Sale | Cost |
| Joint venture | Spin off | Balance sheet |
| Acquisition | | Reorganization |

A **leveraged buyout** (LBO) combines all three. The financial impact rises from an equity investment toward an LBO.

### Investment

* **Equity investment**: buy a material stake **below 50%**; both companies stay independent; the investor may get board seats. Used for strategic partnerships, as a first step to an acquisition, or to buy an undervalued company.
* **Joint venture**: two or more companies form and **jointly control a new, separate company**, each contributing assets, staff or know-how and sharing its profit or loss. Technically an equity investment, but usually bigger (size, operational control, management time). Common for entering new, often foreign, markets with a local partner.
* **Acquisition**: buy most or all of a target's shares (or a segment, or assets) for cash, stock and/or assumed liabilities. The target becomes a subsidiary and is **consolidated**; only an acquisition gives **full control**.

### Divestment

* **Sale (divestiture)**: the other side of an acquisition. Control passes to the buyer, and the seller gets cash to reinvest or return to shareholders or creditors; both sides focus on their strengths.
* **Spin off**: separate a distinct business into a new, independent company, usually by distributing its shares to the parent's shareholders. It removes incompatibilities, sharpens management and employee focus, and allows stock-based pay tied to each business.

| | Sale | Spin off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proceeds to parent | **Cash** (liquidity) | Little; shareholders receive the shares |
| Valuation | Often higher for a mid-size unit with many bidders; **certain** | Set by the market; investors must value it |
| Time | Varies | Several quarters (new management, legal and finance functions) |
| Regulatory scrutiny | Antitrust review of the buyer | Light (reduces concentration) |

### Restructuring

**Forced restructuring:**

* **Cost restructuring**: cut costs to improve efficiency and profitability, often back to historical or peer margins. Follows underperformance, is part of capturing post-deal synergies, or defends against activists or bidders.
  * **Outsourcing**: hand standardized processes (IT, call centers, HR, legal, finance, manufacturing, e.g., iPhones at Hon Hai) to specialists with scale; adds contract risk.
  * **Offshoring**: move operations to another country **within** the company (lower labor costs, centralization). Often combined with outsourcing.
* **Balance sheet restructuring**: change the asset mix, the capital structure, or both.
  * **Sale leaseback**: sell an asset to a lessor and immediately lease it back, often for its remaining life. It unlocks value in non-core assets, raises cash quickly (airlines during COVID-19), repays debt and can improve the credit rating. The lease cost is usually **higher** than the depreciation it replaces (the lessor earns interest): the seller swaps ownership risks for higher, less predictable operating costs.
  * **Dividend recapitalization**: borrow to pay a dividend or buy back shares, shifting from equity to cheaper debt to **lower WACC**. Works when rates are low; raises leverage, so it suits issuers with stable revenue and operating cash flow.
* **Reorganization**: a court-supervised restructuring of an insolvent company. The court oversees negotiations with creditors (asset sales, debt-for-equity swaps, refinancing); operations and management usually continue, and the plan needs court approval. If terms are agreed with creditors before filing, approval can be fast (some have taken under 24 hours). **Liquidation** follows a failed reorganization: the court sells the assets and pays creditors from the proceeds. E.g., Six Flags (Chapter 11, 2009): bondholders swapped over USD1bn of debt for equity, and the old shareholders lost everything.

**Opportunistic restructuring:** the same cost and balance sheet actions, plus changing the **business model**, e.g., **franchising**: the owner sells its assets and licenses its intellectual property to franchisees for royalties, typically a mid-single-digit % of sales (McDonald's, Kumon). The franchisor runs a lean, fixed-cost business and shifts store-level risk to franchisees.

### Leveraged Buyouts

A **private equity** fund (not a company) buys a target using a lot of debt, restructures it, and aims to exit through a sale or a public listing. If the target was listed, it is a **take-private**. Returns depend on the purchase price, the leverage, the free cash flow during ownership (used to repay debt) and the exit price. The target usually exits **more levered** than before: in Blackstone's 2007 take-private of Hilton (USD5.5bn of equity in a USD26bn deal), long-term debt rose from about USD7bn to USD21bn.

**Points worth remembering:**

* Acquisition vs internal investment: faster **time to market**, but **costly** (a price above replacement cost plus a control premium).
* Least likely divestment motive: **increasing revenue**.
* Matching cases to actions: negative operating cash flow with debt coming due → **reorganization**; saturated markets → **acquisition**; one segment hit by regulation → **spin off**; capital- and labor-intensive clinics → **balance sheet restructuring** (franchising, sale leaseback).
* Reason to **sell** rather than spin off: the issuer needs **liquidity**.
* At least two-thirds of acquisitions fail to create meaningful value, because of **overpaying**, **under-delivered synergies** and **integration problems**.

#### Exam traps

* An equity investment is **below 50%**, with no control; a JV is a **new**, jointly controlled company; only an acquisition is consolidated.
* Opportunistic restructuring includes franchising; forced restructuring is cost, balance sheet or reorganization.
* Liquidation ≠ reorganization: a reorganization keeps the business running.

## Evaluating a Restructuring

The analyst works in four steps: **initial evaluation**, **preliminary valuation**, **modeling and valuation**, then **updating the investment thesis**.

### Initial Evaluation

* **What and why**: read the press release, filings, call transcripts and third-party research, with **professional skepticism** (management always presents a deal positively).
* **Is it material?** Look at **size** and **fit**.

$$
\text{Relative size}=\frac{\text{Deal value}}{\text{Acquirer's EV}}
$$

  * **Size**: deal value = cash + stock issued + target debt assumed, and the acquirer's EV is pre-deal. Rule of thumb: **large** if above **10%**. For a cost restructuring, the cost cut as a % of revenue or operating expenses. Most deals (over 95%) are under USD1bn, and over 80% of targets are private, so most deals don't matter for large companies.
  * **Fit**: a small deal can still matter if it signals a change in strategy. E.g., **Farfetch** bought New Guards Group for USD704m, about 8% of its EV, moving from an asset-light marketplace to owning brands with inventory risk; its shares fell 45% the next day.
* **When?** Closing can take several quarters, or over a year, and the deal enters the statements only at **closing**. Shareholder, creditor and regulatory (antitrust) approvals add uncertainty. Markets price in the expected effect, including the risk of not closing, at announcement.

> [!NOTE]
> Announcement-day returns are a poor guide: Rehm and West (2016) found no correlation with excess returns two or more years later, and more than half of deals with negative initial reactions later earned excess returns.

### Preliminary Valuation

Use relative valuation here; the DCF comes in the modeling step.

* **Comparable company analysis**: apply the trading multiples of similar listed companies.
  * Define a peer set (management's peer group or a data provider's; similar size, growth, margins and ROIC; as many as possible without diluting it).
  * Compute EV/EBITDA, EV/sales, P/E (less often EV/FCFF) or sector multiples (EV/subscribers, EV/reserves, EV/FFO), and take the mean, median and range.
  * It gives a fair **trading** price, so **add a takeover premium** for a takeover price. Used more for **spin offs** than for acquisitions.
* **Comparable transaction analysis**: apply multiples from past acquisitions of similar targets. The takeover premium is **already included**: don't add one.
* **Premium paid analysis**: the takeover premium is

$$
PRM=\frac{DP-SP}{SP}
$$

  where $DP$ is the deal price per share and $SP$ the **unaffected** share price. Exclude run-ups driven by rumors: use the price a week or more before the announcement, or a one-week or one-month VWAP. The median annual premium from 1990 to 2018 was just over **30%**, in a range of 20%–40%.

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Comparable company** | Sensible relative value ("like assets valued alike"); data readily available; taken from the market, with few assumptions | Hard to find comparables for unique leaders (e.g., YouTube); sensitive to **market mispricing**; gives a trading price, so needs a premium |
| **Comparable transaction** | Based on actual deal prices; premium included | Few, illiquid deals; old multiples reflect old industry and market conditions; past buyers may have over- or underpaid (bidding wars) |

#### Example: Premium Paid

The unaffected price is USD40 (a week before rumors), and the deal price USD52.

$$PRM=\frac{52-40}{40}=30\%$$

Measuring from a rumor-inflated USD46 would show only $(52-46)/46=13\%$, understating the premium.

#### Example: Wang (Spin Off of Media)

Wang's current EV is EUR96,380m (10× EBITDA). Value each segment at its own peer multiple:

$$
\begin{aligned}
EV_{\text{SOTP}}&=\underbrace{7{,}638\times 13}_{\text{Connectivity}}+\underbrace{2{,}000\times 6}_{\text{Media}}\\
&=99{,}294+12{,}000=111{,}294
\end{aligned}
$$

That is more than 15% above the current EV, so a spin off **could add** value. But:

* Media may not earn the peer multiple (growth, profitability, risk; years of underinvestment).
* Allocate EUR250m of head-office costs by revenue: Media's share is $8{,}000/28{,}100=28.5\%$, or 71m, so its adjusted EBITDA is $2{,}000-71=$ **1,929m**.
* EV multiples don't depend on how much debt the spin off takes (though very high leverage could raise its cost of capital).

#### Example: Hofer Buys Medical Services at USD55

| Multiple | Peer mean | × Medical Services | Value |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| P/E | 18.3 | EPS 2.62 | 47.95 |
| P/CF | 10.2 | CFPS 4.33 | 44.17 |
| P/BV | 3.7 | BVPS 12.65 | 46.81 |
| P/S | 2.4 | SPS 22.98 | 55.15 |

Weighting the four values 20/40/20/20:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V={}&0.2(47.95)+0.4(44.17)\\
&+0.2(46.81)+0.2(55.15)\\
={}&47.65
\end{aligned}
$$

**USD47.65** is 13% below the USD55 paid: Hofer **overpaid**.

### Modeling and Valuation

Build **pro forma** financial statements that include the restructuring, then compute ratios (EPS, net debt/EBITDA, FCF) and a DCF at a **pro forma WACC**.

For an acquisition: combine the acquirer and target → add the financing (borrowing, interest, share issuance) → add synergies or dis-synergies → remove any divestitures (voluntary or required by regulators) → apply accounting and tax effects (goodwill, fair value adjustments).

| Line | How to build it |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue | Combine; add revenue synergies or subtract dis-synergies |
| Operating expenses | Combine; subtract cost synergies (add dis-synergies and one-time costs) |
| D&A | Combine; **add amortization of acquired intangibles** |
| Other income/expense | Combine |
| Interest expense | Acquirer's current interest + interest on new debt (or at a revised rate) |
| Income taxes | EBT-weighted average of the two tax rates (usually given) |
| Shares outstanding | Acquirer's shares + any new shares issued |

**Pro forma WACC.** A restructuring changes the **weights** (a debt-financed cash deal raises $w_d$; selling a unit to repay debt raises $w_e$) and the **costs**, through these drivers:

| Factor | Primary measures |
| --- | --- |
| Profitability | EBITDA or EBIT to sales |
| Volatility | Standard deviation of revenue and of EBITDA |
| Leverage | Debt to EBITDA |
| Collateral | Asset specificity, liquidity, an active market for the asset |
| Interest rates | Market reference rates, corporate credit spreads |

Falling from investment grade to speculative grade adds several hundred bp to WACC (median US large-cap WACC about 4% at AAA/AA, 4.5% at BBB, 6.5% at B), so issuers structure deals to **keep an investment-grade rating**.

#### Example: Kansas City Southern (CP vs CN Bids)

| | CP | CN |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Offer per KCS share | USD274 (23% premium): 0.489 CP shares + USD90 cash | USD325 (45% premium): 1.129 CN shares + USD200 cash |
| Enterprise value | USD29bn | USD33.6bn |
| New debt | USD8.6bn | USD19bn |
| Debt/EBITDA | 4.0× | 4.6× |
| KCS holders' share of the combined company | 25% | 12.6% |

**Step 1. CN's capital structure** (USD bn; CN's share price 105). Combined debt after the deal is 33, including KCS's assumed debt of 3.8 and the new 19:

| | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Debt | $33-3.8-19=10.2$ | 33 |
| Equity | $713\text{m}\times 105=74.9$ | $(713+103)\text{m}\times 105=85.7$ |
| **Debt / equity weights** | **12% / 88%** | **28% / 72%** |

**Step 2. Compare the offers.**

* More stock in the offer → KCS holders own more of the combined company.
* CN funds its bigger cash component with **more debt**; the least attractive feature for KCS holders is CN's **higher leverage** (4.6×). To protect its rating, CN should use **more stock**.

**Step 3. CN's pro forma EPS.** Synergies 333/667/1,000; interest of 5% × USD33bn = 1,650; USD800m of intangible amortization; 22% tax; 103m new shares.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Standalone EPS | 6.16 | 6.41 | 6.74 |
| Pro forma EPS | **5.24** | **5.89** | **6.60** |

The deal is **dilutive** in every year.

#### Exam traps

* Comparable **company** → add a takeover premium; comparable **transaction** → the premium is already in the multiple.
* Use the **unaffected** price for the premium.
* Pro forma D&A includes amortization of **acquired intangibles**; pro forma shares include **new shares issued**.
* An investment-grade rating is worth protecting: more stock in the consideration → less debt.

## Evaluating Investment Actions

#### Example: Dilmun Buys 30% of Spina (Equity Investment)

Tobacco company Dilmun (falling volumes, ESG pressure) buys **30%** of e-vapor leader Spina for USD1.2bn, borrowing at 6%, plus service agreements (shelf space, marketing). Spina's EV is USD4.0bn, and it stays independent.

**Step 1. Why this form?**

* Alternatives: an **acquisition** (control, but far more capital in a risky target) or a **JV** (board seats, but more capital and less independence for Spina).
* Motives: Dilmun gets exposure to growth and Spina's capabilities, diversifying away from cigarettes; Spina gets marketing synergies and cash while keeping control.

**Step 2. Price.**

$$\frac{EV}{\text{Sales}}=\frac{4{,}000}{600}=6.7\times$$

That is the second highest against peers (median 5.1×, average 6.1×); growth and lower risk may explain it.

**Step 3. Impact on 20X4E** (USD m). Spina expects a loss of 450:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{EBIT hit}&=\underbrace{0.3\times 450}_{\text{equity method}}+\underbrace{10}_{\text{amort.}}\\
&=145\\
\text{Interest}&=1{,}200\times 6\%=72
\end{aligned}
$$

| | Standalone | With Spina |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Net income (after a tax shield of 46) | 7,256 | 7,084 |
| EPS | 3.94 | **3.85** |
| Debt/EBITDA | $13{,}894/10{,}140=1.37$ | $15{,}094/9{,}995=$ **1.51** |

#### Example: Opone–Hapalla JV (OHAA)

Opone and Hapalla each own 50% of OHAA. Opone's share of JV income is more than 100% of its profit; Hapalla gets to expand and share risk with a local partner.

| Opone | Opone | Peers |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| P/E | **21** | 12 |
| P/FCF | **7** | 17 |

The P/FCF is low because the JV's dividends (20,000 received in 20X7) far exceed the JV income Opone recognizes (7,634).

**Step 1. Value implied by Hapalla's offer** of BRL45bn for another **25%**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\text{OHAA}}&=\frac{45}{0.25}=180\text{bn}\\
\text{Equity}&=180{,}000-\underbrace{32{,}461}_{\text{cash}}\\
&=147{,}539\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is about **10×** 20X7 profit, against 12× for peers.

**Step 2. Opone's 20X8E.** It sells half of its stake, carried at BRL26bn:

$$\text{Gain}=45-\underbrace{26/2}_{13}=\text{BRL}32\text{bn}$$

JV income falls from 9,379 to 4,689; with 10% tax, profit after tax goes from **7,761 to 31,735**.

**Step 3. Hapalla's accounting** moves from the **equity method to consolidation** (75%): it records a **non-controlling interest** for Opone's 25%, derecognizes the JV investment, and recognizes OHAA's assets and liabilities.

#### Example: Tulor Buys Caracol's Retail Segment

Tulor pays AUD2bn in cash plus 80m shares, AUD3bn in all. Caracol uses AUD1.6bn of after-tax proceeds to repay debt and refocuses on its upstream and downstream businesses. Tulor gets growth and synergies of AUD125m by year 3 (over 15% of its EBITDA).

**Step 1. Price.** Retail's EBITDA is 183:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{EV}{EBITDA}&=\frac{3{,}000}{183}=16\times\\
\text{With synergies}&=\frac{3{,}000}{183+125}=10\times
\end{aligned}
$$

16× is double the peer median of 8× (a control premium plus synergies); with synergies, 10× is within the peer range.

**Step 2. Tulor's 20X3E** (synergies 42, amortization 200, 0.5% on cash, 6% on debt, 20% tax; AUD m):

| | Standalone | Pro forma |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| EBITDA | 1,803 | 2,035 |
| D&A | 652 | 960 |
| Interest income | 24 | 19 |
| Interest expense | 401 | 461 |
| Net income | 620 | 506 |
| Shares (m) | 648 | 728 |
| **EPS** | $620/648=0.96$ | $506/728=$ **0.70** |
| **Debt/EBITDA** | $6{,}169/1{,}803=3.4$ | $7{,}169/2{,}035=$ **3.5** |

EBITDA rises, but EPS falls: amortization, interest and new shares.

#### Exam traps

* An equity-method investee's losses hit the investor's operating income at its **ownership %**, plus any amortization of fair value adjustments.
* Raising a JV stake to control changes the accounting from the equity method to **consolidation**, with a non-controlling interest.
* An acquisition can raise EBITDA yet cut EPS (amortization, interest, new shares).

## Evaluating Divestment Actions

Companies may announce a **strategic review** before acting; analysts then weight the possible outcomes by likelihood, because markets price risk-adjusted outcomes at announcement.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Conglomerate discount}\\
=EV_{\text{SOTP}}-EV_{\text{current}}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Example: Benefit Ltd.

| ZAR m | Consulting | BenefitsExchange (software) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| LTM revenue | | 75 (growing 36.4%) |
| LTM EBITDA | 84 | −5 |

Corporate costs are 4; the market EV is **ZAR1,437m**. An activist holds 8%.

**Step 1. Sum of the parts** (Consulting bears the corporate costs):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Consulting}&=(84-4)\times\underbrace{9}_{\text{EV/EBITDA}}\\
&\approx 721\\
\text{BenefitsEx.}&=75\times\underbrace{12}_{\text{EV/sales}}=900\\
\text{Discount}&=1{,}621-1{,}437=184
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the peer with the closest growth (35%, EV/sales 15) instead: $75\times 15=1{,}125$, a total of 1,846 and a discount of **409**.

**Step 2. A ZAR800m bid for Consulting.**

* $800/84\approx 10\times$, against a comparable-transaction median and mean of **11×**: moderately low.
* But the market implicitly values Consulting at $1{,}437-1{,}125=$ **312**, so the bid is **488** above that.

**Step 3. Sale plus a buyback.** ZAR800m buys back 200m shares; corporate costs fall by 1 and D&A by 12; 0% tax. EBIT becomes −16 and net income **−25** on 1,254m shares: EPS goes from +2.41 cents to **−1.99 cents**, so the deal is dilutive.

**Step 4. Sale vs spin off.** If a spin off would trade at **13×**, it beats selling at 10×; but a sale price is **certain**, while a spin off's value can fall in a market correction.

#### Exam traps

* Conglomerate discount = sum-of-the-parts EV − **current** EV (positive = discount).
* Allocate corporate costs before applying a multiple if the question says so.
* Value each segment at its **own** multiple; applying the average multiple to total EBITDA ignores each segment's size.
* Selling a profitable unit and buying back stock can still be **dilutive** if the remaining business earns little.

## Evaluating Restructuring Actions

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Cut}={}&\text{Opex}\\
&-(1-\text{Target margin})\\
&\times\text{Revenue}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Example: Cyrene SARL (Cost Restructuring)

Cyrene rejected a 20%-premium bid from an aggressive cost-cutter; shareholders warned that as long as it didn't act to increase shareholder value, it would stay vulnerable to an acquirer who would. Below-average profitability makes it cheap to buy once synergies are counted.

**Step 1. The gap to peers** (EUR m; revenue 18,990, operating expenses 16,331, EBIT 2,659):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Margin}&=\frac{2{,}659}{18{,}990}=14\%\\
\text{Cut}&=16{,}331-0.80\times 18{,}990\\
&=16{,}331-15{,}192\\
&=1{,}139
\end{aligned}
$$

Cyrene's 14% margin is 6 points below peers' 20%. The cut is **EUR1,139m (7%)**.

**Step 2. Next year**, closing the gap at 1.5 points of margin a year over four years, with 3% revenue growth ($EBIT_{\text{pre}}$ is before the one-time costs):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Revenue}&=18{,}990\times 1.03=19{,}560\\
EBIT_{\text{pre}}&=15.5\%\times 19{,}560=3{,}032\\
EBIT&=3{,}032-1{,}250\\
&=1{,}782\ (9.1\%)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3. Risks.** Cutting spending that drives its above-peer **growth** (4%), and **political** backlash from layoffs and closures.

**Alternative.** Selling or spinning off Household Goods (EBIT 496) leaves EBIT of 2,163: a **16%** margin (+2 points), but EBIT **−19%**. To judge it, the analyst needs the unit's sale or spin off value, the effects on the remaining segments, the corporate cost savings, and the details of the cost plan.

$$
\text{Cap rate}=\frac{\text{Annual rent (NOI)}}{\text{Sale price}}
$$

#### Example: Kosala Corp. (Sale Leaseback of Distribution Centers)

| Term | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Proceeds | CHF425m: repay CHF215m of debt, buy back 10m shares |
| Rent | CHF19m a year (Kosala keeps paying maintenance, taxes and utilities) |
| Gain on sale | CHF200m, amortized over 15 years |
| Savings | Depreciation 30; interest 15 |
| Right-of-use asset and lease liability | CHF198m |

**Step 1. Motives.** Unlock value in **non-core** real estate at an attractive cap rate; repay debt and aim for an **investment-grade** rating.

**Step 2. Is the price good?**

$$\text{Cap rate}=\frac{19}{425}=4.5\%$$

Comparables' 25th/50th/75th percentiles are 3.0%/5.5%/8.0%, so Kosala's is **100 bp below the median**: favorable, since a lower cap rate means a higher price. Location and physical condition drive cap rates.

**Step 3. Pro forma** (25% tax; CHF m). SG&A rises by the rent of 19, and D&A is $67-30+13=50$ (the textbook adds the 13 of gain amortization).

| | Before | After |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| EBIT | 124 | 122 |
| Interest | 43 | 28 |
| Net income | 61 | 70 |
| Shares (m) | 97 | 87 |
| **EPS** | 0.63 | **0.81** |
| **Debt/EBITDA** | $615/191=3.2$ | $400/172=$ **2.3** |
| **EBIT/interest** | 2.9 | **4.3** |

**Step 4. Rating.** On the rating grid, 2.3× and 4.3× map to **BBB**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Interest}&=(4.50\%+1.25\%)\times 400\\
&=\text{CHF}23\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

That is CHF5m less than 28.

#### Exam traps

* A **lower** cap rate means a **higher** sale price: good for the seller-lessee.
* In a sale leaseback, depreciation on the sold assets falls, rent rises, and repaying debt cuts interest.
* Required cost cut for a target margin = operating expenses − (1 − target margin) × revenue.

> [!NOTE]
>
> **Textbook issues.**
>
> * OHAA: the solution **subtracts** cash from the BRL180bn to get the equity value. The usual bridge is equity = EV − debt + cash, so the step looks reversed; and 147,539/15,267 = 9.7×, which the text rounds to 10×. Follow the given numbers if the exam reuses them.
> * Kosala: the solution adds the 13 of gain amortization to D&A (67 − 30 + 13 = 50), which the EBIT of 122 relies on. A deferred gain would normally **reduce** expense; 13 also equals the right-of-use asset's depreciation (198/15), which is the more natural reading of the +13.
> * Benefit: (84 − 4) × 9 = 720; the textbook's 721 (and so 1,621, 184, 1,846 and 409) presumably comes from unrounded EBITDA.
> * Tulor: the solution says the share issue "is alone responsible for a loss of AUD0.26 per share". But 0.26 is the **total** EPS drop: spreading the old 620 of net income over 728 shares costs only 0.96 − 0.85 = 0.11; the rest comes from amortization and interest.
