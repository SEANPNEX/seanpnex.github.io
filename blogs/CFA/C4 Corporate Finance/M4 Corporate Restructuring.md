The candidate should be able to:

* Explain the initial evaluation of a corporate restructuring

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |

## Classification of Restructuring

Companies move through a life cycle (start-up → growth → maturity → decline). Revenue growth slows and FCF peaks at maturity; debt rises from about 0% at start-up to 20%+ at maturity. Managers act to change that destiny:

* Investment
  * Increase the size or scope of a company, thereby increasing the revenue and perhaps revenue growth (here: **external/inorganic** growth, not capex or R&D)
* Divestment
  * Reduce the size or scope of a company, typically shedding slower-growing, lower-profitability or higher-risk operations
* Restructuring
  * Improve the cost and financing structure to increase growth, improve profitability, or reduce risks **without** altering the size or scope of the company

**Motivation** (issuer-specific)

* Investment action motivations
  * Realize synergies
    * Cost synergies: G&A (one headquarters), manufacturing and distribution (capacity utilization, route density), R&D, sales and marketing
    * Revenue synergies: cross-selling (bank + insurer), bargaining power from reduced competition
  * Increase growth
  * Improve capabilities or secure resources (vertical integration lowers costs and risk; cross-border deals for new markets, facilities, talent)
  * Acquire undervalued targets

* Divest
  * Focus operations and business lines (e.g. Daimler spin off of Daimler Truck, 2021)
  * Valuation
    * Conglomerate discount: from diseconomies of scale or scope, incompatible businesses, or markets overlooking a business. The divested unit may be worth more outside (e.g. Novartis spun off Alcon in 2019 at over 30× EPS vs Novartis about half that; Alcon +35% in two years, Novartis flat)
  * Liquidity needs: sell businesses to cut unsustainable leverage (often at low prices, good for the buyer)
  * Regulatory requirements: antitrust conditions on a deal, or court-ordered divestiture
* Restructuring
  * Improve returns on capital
    * Opportunistic improvement: alter the business model, trim costs, change the balance sheet
    * Forced improvement: when returns fall below the required return (weak management, falling demand, competition, overcapacity)
  * Financial challenges, including bankruptcy and liquidation

**Motivations: Top-down**

* High security prices: all types of change are **pro-cyclical** (BCG: correlation 0.80 between MSCI World and deal volume, 2000–2019). Explanations:
  * Greater CEO confidence
  * Lower cost of financing (lower rates, lower ERP → less interest and less dilution)
  * Management knows its stock is **overvalued** → use it as currency in acquisitions, sales and spin offs
* Industry shocks: regulatory changes, technological changes, changes in industry growth → industry-specific waves

> [!note]
> Deals done in **weak** economies created more value on average (BCG: nearly 10% higher three-year shareholder return than "strong economy" deals).

## Types of corporate restructuring

|                  | **Investment (increase size)** | **Divestment (decrease size)** | **Restructuring (improve)**                  |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Types**        | Equity investment              | Sale                           | Cost                                         |
|                  | Joint venture                  | Spin off                       | Balance sheet                                |
|                  | Acquisition                    |                                | Reorganization                               |
| **Special case** | Leveraged buyout (LBO): combines investment, divestment and restructuring |  |  |

Financial impact increases from equity investment down to LBO.

### Investment

* Equity investment: buy a material stake **below 50%**; both stay independent; may get board seats. Used for strategic partnerships, as a first step to an acquisition, or to buy an undervalued company
* Joint venture: two or more companies form and **jointly control a new, separate company**, each contributing assets, staff or know-how and sharing profit/loss. Technically an equity investment but usually larger (size, operational control, management time). Common for entering new (often foreign) markets with a local partner
* Acquisition: buy most or all of the target's shares (or a segment or assets) for cash, stock and/or assumed liabilities. The target becomes a subsidiary and is **consolidated**; only an acquisition gives **full control**

### Divest

* Sales/ Divestiture: the other side of an acquisition; control passes to the buyer, seller gets cash
  * Reallocation of capital (or return it to shareholders or creditors)
  * The seller and acquirer focus on strength
* Spin off: A company separates a distinct part of its business into a new, independent company (usually distributed to the parent's shareholders as a stock dividend)
  * Remove incompatibilities, and increase management and employee focus
  * Separating distinct businesses
  * Awarding employees with stock-based compensation tied to their own business

| | Sale | Spin off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proceeds to parent | **Cash** (liquidity) | Little; shareholders receive the shares |
| Valuation | Often higher for a moderate-size unit with many bidders; **definitive** | Set by the market; investors must value it |
| Time | Varies | Several quarters (new management, legal, finance functions) |
| Regulatory scrutiny | Antitrust review of the buyer | Light (reduces concentration) |

### Restructuring (Forced)

* Cost restructuring: Reduce costs by improving operational efficiency and profitability, often to historical or peer margins; follows underperformance, part of post-deal synergy capture, or a defense against activists or bidders
  * Outsourcing: subcontract standardizable processes (IT, call centers, HR, legal, finance, manufacturing, e.g. iPhones at Hon Hai) to specialists with scale; adds contract risks
  * Offshoring: relocate operations to another country **within** the company (lower labor cost, centralization). The two are often combined
* Balance sheet restructuring: Shift the asset composition, change the capital structure, or both
  * Sale leaseback: sell an asset to a lessor and immediately lease it back (often for its remaining economic life). Unlock value in non-core assets, raise cash quickly (airlines in COVID-19), retire debt, improve the credit rating. Lease expense is usually **higher** than the depreciation it replaces (the lessor earns interest); the seller swaps ownership risks for higher, less predictable operating costs
  * Dividend recapitalization: debt-financed dividends or buybacks, shifting equity to cheaper debt to **lower WACC**. Works when rates are low; raises leverage, so suits issuers with stable revenue and operating cash flow
* Reorganization: A court-supervised restructuring process for insolvent companies. The court oversees negotiation with creditors (asset sales, debt-to-equity conversion, refinancing); operations and management usually continue; the plan needs court approval. If the company agrees terms with creditors before filing, approval can be quick (some reorganizations have lasted under 24 hours)
  * **Liquidation** follows a failed reorganization: the court sells the assets and distributes proceeds to creditors

**Textbook example (Six Flags).** Fixed costs → EBIT −50% in 2006 and −34% in 2007; net debt/EBITDA about 12–13×. Cost restructuring in 2008 couldn't stop defaults; Chapter 11 in June 2009. Bondholders invested USD725m and converted over USD1bn of debt to equity; emerged with USD784m net debt (< 3.0× 2010 EBITDA). **Old shareholders lost everything.**

### Restructuring (Opportunistic)

* Alter the business model
  * Franchising: An owner can divest its asset and license the intellectual property to a third party operator (franchisee) for royalties, typically mid-single-digit % of sales (McDonald's, Kumon). The franchisor runs a lean, fixed-cost business and shifts store-level risks to franchisees
* Cost restructuring
* Balance sheet restructuring

### Leveraged Buyout

An acquirer (a **private equity** fund, not a corporate) uses a significant amount of debt to finance the acquisition of a target and then pursues restructuring actions, with the goal of exiting the target with a sale or public listing. It can be a combination of all methods above. A listed target becomes a **take-private**. Returns depend on: purchase price, leverage, FCF during ownership (used to repay debt), and exit price. The target usually exits **more levered** than before.

**Textbook example (Hilton / Blackstone, 2007).** About USD26bn: USD47.50 a share (≈ USD20bn) plus USD6bn assumed debt; financed with USD14.5bn borrowing and USD5.5bn equity. Long-term debt went from about USD7bn to USD21bn (×3). Growth via franchising, sale of flagship properties; re-IPO 2013; over USD11bn cumulative net profit on USD5.5bn by 2018.

**Textbook answers worth remembering.**

* Acquisition vs internal investment: faster **time to market**, but **costly** (prices above replacement cost plus a control premium).
* Least likely divestment motive: **increasing revenue**.
* Match: negative CFO with debt due → **reorganization**; saturated markets → **acquisition**; one segment hit by regulation → **spin off**; capital- and labor-intensive clinics → **balance sheet restructuring** (franchising, sale leaseback).
* Reason to **sell** rather than spin off: the issuer needs **liquidity**.
* At least two-thirds of acquisitions fail to create meaningful value: **overpaying**, **under-realized synergies**, **integration problems**.

**Textbook example (AstraZeneca / Alexion).** USD500m cost synergies = 500/2,646 = **19%** of Alexion's year-3 opex. With synergies 166/333/500 and one-time costs 217/433/650, combined operating income = **7,799 / 9,125 / 10,613**. Revenue growth rises about **200 bp** (10%/9% → 12%/11%) and operating margin **400–500 bp** (26–28% → 30–32%), because Alexion grows faster at higher margins.

#### Exam traps

* An equity investment is **< 50%** with no control; a JV is a **new** jointly controlled company; only an acquisition consolidates.
* Opportunistic restructuring includes franchising; forced restructuring = cost, balance sheet, reorganization.
* Liquidation ≠ reorganization: reorganization keeps the business running.

## General Steps of Restructuring

* Initial Evaluation
* Preliminary Valuation
* Modelling and Valuation
* Update the investment thesis

### Initial Evaluation

* What and Why: press release, filings, call transcripts, third-party research; apply **professional skepticism** (management always frames it positively)
* Is it material: **size** and **fit**
  * Size: for a transaction, transaction value (cash + stock issued + target debt assumed) ÷ acquirer's EV. Rule of thumb: **large** if it exceeds $10\%$ of the acquirer's pre-deal enterprise value. For a cost restructuring, the cost cut as % of revenue or opex. Most deals (> 95%) are under USD1bn and > 80% of targets are private → most deals are immaterial for large caps
  * Fit: a small deal can still matter if it signals a change in strategy (e.g. **Farfetch** bought New Guards Group for USD704m, about 8% of EV, moving from asset-light marketplace to own brands with inventory risk; shares −45% the next day)
* When: closing can take several quarters or more than 12 months; the deal hits the statements only at **closing**. Uncertainty from shareholder, creditor and regulatory (antitrust) approvals. Markets price in the expected effect, including the risk of not closing, at announcement

> [!note]
> Announcement-day returns are a poor guide: Rehm and West (2016) found no correlation with excess returns two or more years later, and more than half of deals with negative initial reactions later earned excess returns.

### Preliminary Valuation

Use relative valuation (DCF comes in the modeling step):

* Comparable company analysis: trading multiples of similar listed companies
  * Define a peer set (management's peer group or a data aggregator; similar size, growth, margin, ROIC; as many as possible without diluting)
  * Compute EV/EBITDA, EV/sales, P/E (less often EV/FCFF) or sector multiples (EV/subscribers, EV/reserves, EV/FFO); take mean, median, range
  * Gives a fair **trading** price → must **add a takeover premium** for a takeover price. Used more for **spin offs** than acquisitions
* Comparable transaction analysis
  * Multiples from past acquisitions of similar targets
  * Takeover premium is **already included**: do **not** add one
* Premium paid analysis
  * Takeover premium
    * The amount by which the per-share takeover price exceeds the unaffected price expressed as a percentage of the unaffected price. $PRM=\frac{DP-SP}{SP}$, where $DP$ is the deal price per share and $SP$ is the **unaffected** stock price per share
    * Exclude rumor-driven run-ups: use the price a week (or longer) before announcement, or a week/month VWAP
    * Median annual premium 1990–2018 just over **30%**, range 20%–40%

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Comparable company** | Reasonable relative value ("like assets valued alike"); data readily available; derived directly from the market, few assumptions | Hard to find comparables (unique leaders, e.g. YouTube); sensitive to **market mispricing**; gives a trading price, needs a premium |
| **Comparable transaction** | Based on actual deal prices; premium embedded | Few, illiquid deals; old multiples reflect old industry and macro conditions; past buyers may have over- or underpaid (bidding wars) |

**Textbook example (Wang, spin off of Media).** Connectivity EBITDA 7,638 × 13 + Media 2,000 × 6 = **EUR111,294m** vs current EV 96,380m (10×) → more than 15% higher → spin off **could increase** value. Media may not get the peer multiple (growth, profitability, risk; years of under-investment). Allocating EUR250m HQ costs by revenue: 8,000/28,100 = 28.5% → 71m → adjusted EBITDA **1,929m**. EV multiples are independent of how much debt the spin off gets (but very high leverage could raise its cost of capital).

**Textbook example (Hofer, Medical Services at USD55).** Mean multiples P/E 18.3, P/CF 10.2, P/BV 3.7, P/S 2.4 applied to EPS 2.62, CFPS 4.33, BVPS 12.65, SPS 22.98 → 47.95, 44.17, 46.81, 55.15. Weights 20/40/20/20 → **USD47.65**, 13% below USD55 → **overpaid**.

**Worked example (added, not from the textbook): premium paid.** Unaffected price USD40 (a week before rumors), deal price USD52 → PRM = (52 − 40)/40 = **30%**. Measuring from a rumor-inflated USD46 would show only (52 − 46)/46 = 13%, understating the premium.

### Modelling and Valuation

It refers to the change in financial statements in the restructuring process (**pro forma** statements), then ratios (EPS, net debt/EBITDA, FCF) and a DCF at a **pro forma WACC**.

Acquisition modeling steps: combine acquirer + target → financing (borrowing, interest, share issuance) → synergies or dis-synergies → divestitures (voluntary or required by regulators) → accounting and tax (goodwill, fair value adjustments) → combined company.

| Line | How to build it |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue | Combine; add revenue synergies or subtract dis-synergies |
| Operating expenses | Combine; subtract cost synergies or add dis-synergies (and one-time costs) |
| D&A | Combine; **add amortization of acquired intangibles** |
| Other income/expense | Combine |
| Interest expense | Acquirer's current interest + new debt / revised rate |
| Income taxes | EBT-weighted average of the two tax rates (usually given) |
| Shares outstanding | Acquirer's shares + any shares issued |

**Pro forma WACC.** A restructuring changes the **weights** (debt-financed cash deal → $w_d$ ↑; selling a unit to repay debt → $w_e$ ↑) and the **costs** through the drivers below. Moving from investment grade to speculative grade adds several hundred bp to WACC (median US large-cap WACC about 4% at AAA/AA, 4.5% at BBB, 6.5% at B), so issuers structure deals to **keep investment grade**.

| Factor | Primary measures |
| --- | --- |
| Profitability | EBITDA or EBIT to sales |
| Volatility | Std dev of revenues, of EBITDA |
| Leverage | Debt to EBITDA |
| Collateral | Asset specificity, liquidity, active market for the asset |
| Interest rates | Market reference rates, corporate credit spreads |

**Textbook example (Kansas City Southern: CP vs CN).** CP: USD274 (23% premium), 0.489 CP shares + USD90 cash, EV USD29bn, USD8.6bn new debt, 4.0× debt/EBITDA, KCS holders own 25%. CN: USD325 (45% premium), 1.129 CN shares + USD200 cash, EV USD33.6bn, USD19bn new debt, 4.6×, KCS holders 12.6%.

* CN debt before = 33 − 3.8 − 19 = USD10.2bn; equity 713m × 105 = USD74.9bn → **12% / 88%**. After: debt 33; equity (713 + 103) × 105 = 85.7 → **28% / 72%**.
* More CN stock in the mix → KCS holders own more. CN funds its bigger cash component by **more debt**. Least attractive for KCS holders: **higher leverage** (4.6×). To protect the rating: use **more stock**.
* Pro forma (synergies 333/667/1,000; 5% on USD33bn = 1,650 interest; USD800m intangible amortization; 22% tax; 103m new shares): EPS **5.24 / 5.89 / 6.60** vs standalone 6.16 / 6.41 / 6.74 → **dilutive**.

#### Exam traps

* Comparable **company** → add a takeover premium; comparable **transaction** → premium already in the multiple.
* Use the **unaffected** price for PRM.
* Pro forma D&A includes amortization of **acquired intangibles**; pro forma shares include **new shares issued**.
* Investment grade is worth protecting: more stock in the consideration → less debt.

## Evaluating Investment Actions

**Textbook example (Dilmun / Spina, equity investment).** Tobacco company Dilmun (declining volumes, ESG pressure) buys **30%** of e-vapor leader Spina for USD1.2bn (EV USD4.0bn), debt-financed at 6%, plus service agreements (shelf space, marketing). Spina stays independent.

* Alternatives: **acquisition** (control, but much more capital into a risky target) or **JV** (governance seats, but more capital and less of Spina's independence).
* Motives: Dilmun wants growth exposure and Spina's capabilities, diversifying from combustibles; Spina wants the marketing synergies and cash while keeping control.
* EV/sales = 4,000/600 = **6.7×** vs peers median 5.1×, average 6.1× (second highest; growth and lower risk may explain it).
* 20X4E: equity-method loss 0.3 × 450 = 135 + 10 amortization = **145** off EBIT/EBITDA; interest 1,200 × 6% = **72**; tax shield 46. NI 7,256 → 7,084; EPS **3.94 → 3.85**; debt/EBITDA 13,894/10,140 = **1.37 → 1.51** (15,094/9,995).

**Textbook example (Opone-Hapalla JV, OHAA).** Opone's JV income (50%) is more than 100% of its profit; Hapalla gets to expand and share risk with a local partner. Opone P/E **21** vs peers 12, but P/FCF **7** vs peers 17: JV dividends (20,000 received in 20X7) far exceed recognized JV income (7,634).

* Hapalla offers BRL45bn for another **25%** → OHAA valued at 45/0.25 = **BRL180bn**; less year-end cash 32,461 → 147,539m equity value ≈ **10×** 20X7 profit (vs peers 12×).
* Opone's 20X8E: de-recognize half the BRL26bn carrying value (13bn) → gain 45 − 13 = **BRL32bn**; JV income 9,379 → 4,689; tax 10% → profit after tax **7,761 → 31,735**.
* Hapalla moves from the **equity method to consolidation** (75%), records a **non-controlling interest** for Opone's 25%, de-recognizes the JV investment and recognizes OHAA's assets and liabilities.

> [!note]
> Textbook issue: the OHAA solution **subtracts** cash from the BRL180bn to get equity value. The usual bridge is equity = EV − debt + cash, so this step looks reversed; and 147,539/15,267 = 9.7×, which the text rounds to 10.0×. Follow the given numbers if the exam reuses them.

**Textbook example (Tulor buys Caracol's Retail segment).** AUD2bn cash + 80m shares = AUD3bn. Caracol: after-tax proceeds 1.6bn to repay debt; refocus on upstream/downstream. Tulor: synergies AUD125m by year 3 (> 15% of its EBITDA) and growth.

* EV/EBITDA paid = 3,000/183 = **16×** vs peer median **8×**: control premium + synergies. With synergies 3,000/(183 + 125) = **10×**, inside the peer range.
* 20X3E (synergies 42, amortization 200, 0.5% on cash, 6% on debt, 20% tax): EBITDA 1,803 → 2,035; D&A 652 → 960; interest income 24 → 19; interest expense 401 → 461; NI 620 → 506; shares 648 → 728. EPS **0.96 → 0.70**; debt/EBITDA 6,169/1,803 = **3.4 → 3.5** (7,169/2,035).

> [!note]
> Textbook issue: the Tulor solution says the share issuance "is alone responsible for a loss of AUD0.26 per share". 0.26 is the **total** EPS drop; spreading the old 620 of NI over 728 shares only costs 0.96 − 0.85 = 0.11. The rest comes from amortization and interest.

#### Exam traps

* Equity-method investee losses hit the investor's operating income at its **ownership %**, plus any amortization of fair value adjustments.
* Raising a JV stake to control changes accounting from equity method to **consolidation** with a non-controlling interest.
* An acquisition can raise EBITDA yet cut EPS (amortization, interest, new shares).

## Evaluating Divestment Actions

Companies may announce a **strategic review** before any action; analysts then weight scenarios by likelihood, because markets price risk-adjusted outcomes at announcement.

**Textbook example (Benefit Ltd.).** Two segments: Consulting (LTM EBITDA 84) and BenefitsExchange (software, revenue 75, growing 36.4%, EBITDA −5); corporate costs 4. Market EV **ZAR1,437m**. An activist holds 8%.

* Sum of the parts: Consulting (84 − 4) × 9 (peer median EV/EBITDA) = **721**; BenefitsExchange 75 × 12 (median EV/sales) = **900** → **1,621** → conglomerate discount **184**.
* Using the peer with the closest growth (35%, EV/S 15): 75 × 15 = 1,125 → total 1,846 → discount **409**.
* ZAR800m bid for Consulting: 800/84 ≈ **10×** vs comparable transactions median/mean **11×** → moderately low. But implied market value of Consulting = 1,437 − 1,125 = **312** → bid is **488** above it.
* Sale + ASR (800 buys back 200m shares; corporate costs −1; D&A −12; 0% tax): EBIT −16, NI **−25**, shares 1,254 → EPS **−1.99 cents** (from +2.41): dilutive.
* If a spin off would trade at **13×**, spin off rather than sell at 10×; but a sale price is **certain**, while a spin off's value can fall in a correction.

**Textbook practice problem (Saturn).** Upstream 14,400 × 19 + Midstream 5,760 × 17 + Downstream 3,840 × 13 = **USD421,440m** vs EV 408,000m. Averaging the multiples (16.33 × 24,000 = 392,000) is the wrong method.

#### Exam traps

* Conglomerate discount = sum-of-the-parts EV − **current** EV (positive = discount).
* Allocate corporate costs before applying a multiple if the question says so.
* Selling a profitable unit and buying back stock can still be **dilutive** when the remaining business earns little.

## Evaluating Restructuring Actions

**Textbook example (Cyrene SARL, cost restructuring).** Rejected a 20% premium bid from an aggressive cost-cutter; shareholders warned "as long as you don't take actions to increase shareholder value, you are vulnerable to an acquirer who will". Below-average profitability makes Cyrene a cheap target after synergies.

* Peer median EBIT margin **20%**; Cyrene 2,659/18,990 = **14%**. Opex 16,331 must fall to 0.80 × 18,990 = 15,192 → cut **EUR1,139m (7%)**.
* Over four years: +1.5% margin a year. NTM revenue 18,990 × 1.03 = 19,560; EBIT ex costs at 15.5% = **3,032**; less one-time costs 1,250 → **1,782 (9.1%)**.
* Risks: cutting spending that drives its above-peer **growth** (4% vs peers); **political** backlash from layoffs and closures.
* Alternative: sell/spin off Household Goods (EBIT 496) → EBIT 2,163, margin **16%** (+2 points) but EBIT **−19%**. Need: the unit's sale/spin value, effects on remaining segments, corporate cost savings, details of the cost plan.

**Textbook example (Kosala Corp., sale leaseback of distribution centers).** Proceeds CHF425m: repay CHF215m debt and buy back 10m shares. Rent CHF19m a year (Kosala keeps maintenance, taxes, utilities); cap rate **4.5%**; gain CHF200m amortized over 15 years; depreciation saved 30; interest saved 15; ROU asset and lease liability CHF198m.

* Motives: unlock value in **non-core** real estate at attractive cap rates; repay debt and aim for an **investment-grade** rating.
* Cap rate 4.5% vs comparable 25th/50th/75th percentiles 3.0%/5.5%/8.0% → **100 bp below the median** → favorable (lower cap rate = higher price). Location and physical condition drive cap rates.
* Pro forma (25% tax): SG&A +19; D&A 67 − 30 + 13 (gain amortization) = **50**; EBIT 124 → 122; interest 43 → 28; NI 61 → 70; shares 97 → 87; EPS **0.63 → 0.81**. Debt/EBITDA 615/191 = **3.2 → 2.3** (400/172); EBIT/interest **2.9 → 4.3**.
* Rating grid: 2.3× and 4.3× → **BBB**, spread 450 bp + Treasury 125 bp = 5.75% × 400 = **CHF23m** interest, CHF5m less than 28.

**Textbook practice problem (Jupiter).** Sale leaseback of 100 owned stores = **balance sheet restructuring**; the better rating should **lower** (not raise) its cost of capital; cap rate 5.50% equals the median (5.00/5.50/6.00) → **supported** by comparables.

#### Exam traps

* A **lower** cap rate means a **higher** sale price: good for the seller-lessee.
* In a sale leaseback, depreciation falls, rent rises, and the amortized gain reduces D&A; debt repayment cuts interest.
* Target margin cut = opex − (1 − target margin) × revenue.

## Quick Recall

* Three categories: investment (equity investment, JV, acquisition), divestment (sale, spin off), restructuring (cost, balance sheet, reorganization); LBO combines all three.
* Issuer motives: synergies, growth, capabilities, undervalued targets / focus, valuation, liquidity, regulation / returns on capital, financial distress. Top-down: high security prices, industry shocks.
* Evaluation: initial (what, why, material: > 10% of EV or a strategic shift; when) → preliminary valuation (comparable company + premium, comparable transaction, $PRM=\frac{DP-SP}{SP}$) → pro forma modeling → update thesis.
* Pro forma: combine, add synergies and one-time costs, add intangible amortization and new interest, add new shares; then EPS, debt/EBITDA, WACC weights and costs.
* Sale = cash and certainty; spin off = market value, focus, little regulatory scrutiny.
* Sale leaseback: cash up, debt down, lease cost up; dividend recap: more debt, lower WACC if stable.
